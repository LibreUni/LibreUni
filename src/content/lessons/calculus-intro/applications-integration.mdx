## Area Between Curves

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(x)\ge g(x)$ there, the region between their graphs has area

$$A=\int_a^b\bigl(f(x)-g(x)\bigr)\,dx.$$

The order is not optional: if the graphs cross, split the interval at every intersection or integrate $|f-g|$. When horizontal slices are simpler, solve for $x$ as a function of $y$ and use $A=\int_c^d (x_{\mathrm{right}}(y)-x_{\mathrm{left}}(y))\,dy$.

The shaded region is the geometric object represented by the integrand:

For $y=x$ and $y=x^2$, the intersections are $x=0,1$, and the line is above the parabola on that interval. Hence

$$A=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac16.$$

**Analysis:**
Directly integrating gives:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} (\sin x - \cos x)\,dx = [-\cos x - \sin x]_0^{2\pi} = (-1 - 0) - (-1 - 0) = 0!$$
The naive algebraic integral yields **zero** because the positive region between $x = \pi/4$ and $x = 5\pi/4$ is exactly cancelled by the opposite regions on $[0, \pi/4]$ and $[5\pi/4, 2\pi]$.
Geometric area is *strictly positive*: one must split at the intersection points $x = \pi/4, 5\pi/4$ to integrate $|f(x) - g(x)|$:
$$A = \int_0^{\pi/4} (\cos x - \sin x)\,dx + \int_{\pi/4}^{5\pi/4} (\sin x - \cos x)\,dx + \int_{5\pi/4}^{2\pi} (\cos x - \sin x)\,dx = 4\sqrt{2} \approx 5.6568.$$

## Volumes by Slicing

Let a solid have cross-sectional area $A(x)$ perpendicular to the $x$-axis. If $A$ is integrable, Cavalieri's slicing principle gives

<MathStatement id="volume-by-slices" kind="theorem" title="Volume by Slicing" number="1">

$$V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx.$$

</MathStatement>

Revolving a region about the $x$-axis produces disks of area $\pi [R(x)]^2$ when there is no hole, and washers of area $\pi([R(x)]^2-[r(x)]^2)$ when the inner radius is $r(x)$. Shells instead use cylindrical strips: revolving a vertical strip of radius $x$ and height $h(x)$ about the $y$-axis gives

$$V=2\pi\int_a^b xh(x)\,dx.$$

For the region under $y=2x-x^2$ on $[0,2]$, rotation about the $x$-axis gives

$$V=\pi\int_0^2(2x-x^2)^2\,dx=\frac{64\pi}{15}.$$

## Arc Length and Surface Area

Approximate a smooth graph by line segments. For $y=f(x)$, the distance element satisfies

$$ds=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}=\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx.$$

Therefore:

<MathStatement id="arc-length" kind="definition" title="Arc Length of a Graph" number="2">

If $f$ is continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$, its graph has length

$$L=\int_a^b\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx.$$

</MathStatement>

Rotating the graph about the $x$-axis sweeps out a band with circumference $2\pi f(x)$, so for a nonnegative $f$,

$$S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx.$$

These integrals frequently have no elementary antiderivative; numerical quadrature is then part of the mathematical solution, not a failure of the model.

### Worked Trace: Exact Arc Length Evaluation for $y = \frac{2}{3} x^{3/2}$

Consider finding the exact arc length of the curve $y = \frac{2}{3} x^{3/2}$ on the interval $[0, 3]$.

1. **Differentiate the Curve**:
   $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{2} x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}.$$

2. **Formulate the Arc Length Differential**:
   $$ds = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx = \sqrt{1 + (\sqrt{x})^2}\,dx = \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx.$$

3. **Set Up the Definite Integral**:
   $$L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx.$$

4. **Integrate via Substitution**:
   Let $u = 1 + x$, so $du = dx$. When $x = 0$, $u = 1$; when $x = 3$, $u = 4$:
   $$L = \int_1^4 u^{1/2}\,du = \left[ \frac{2}{3} u^{3/2} \right]_1^4 = \frac{2}{3} (4^{3/2} - 1^{3/2}) = \frac{2}{3} (8 - 1) = \frac{14}{3} \approx 4.6667.$$


### Counterexample & Failure Mode: Gabriel's Horn (Finite Volume, Infinite Surface Area)

When integration applications are extended to unbounded domains, geometric intuition can break down dramatically. Consider revolving $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ on the interval $[1, \infty)$ about the $x$-axis.

1. **Volume via Disk Slicing**:
   $$V = \pi \int_1^\infty [f(x)]^2\,dx = \pi \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \pi \lim_{b \to \infty} \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_1^b = \pi \lim_{b \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{b} \right) = \pi.$$
   The volume is finite! It could be filled with exactly $\pi$ cubic units of paint.

2. **Surface Area via Revolving Bands**:
   $$S = 2\pi \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x} \sqrt{1 + \left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right)^2}\,dx = 2\pi \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x} \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^4}}\,dx.$$
   Because $\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^4}} > 1$ for all $x \ge 1$:
   $$S > 2\pi \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x}\,dx = 2\pi \lim_{b \to \infty} [\ln x]_1^b = 2\pi \lim_{b \to \infty} \ln b = \infty.$$

3. **The Painter's Paradox Breakdown**:
   The surface area diverges to infinity while the volume remains strictly finite ($\pi$). This paradox demonstrates that three-dimensional measure (volume $\sim \int x^{-2}$) converges much faster than two-dimensional boundary measure (surface area $\sim \int x^{-1}$), warning against conflating surface and volume scalings on unbounded regimes.


## Mass, Work, and Average Value

For a one-dimensional object with linear density $\rho(x)$, a slice of width $dx$ has mass $dm=\rho(x)\,dx$, giving

$$m=\int_a^b\rho(x)\,dx.$$

If a variable force $F(x)$ moves an object from $a$ to $b$ in the positive direction, the work is

$$W=\int_a^bF(x)\,dx.$$

The same accumulation logic yields the average value of a continuous function:

$$f_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

**Worked example.** A two-metre rod has density $\rho(x)=1+x$ kg/m, where $x$ is measured in metres from one end. Its mass is $\int_0^2(1+x)\,dx=[x+x^2/2]_0^2=4$ kg. Its average density is $4/2=2$ kg/m. Dividing by length is appropriate for average density, not for the total mass.

## Deliberate Omissions & Course Scope Boundaries

* **Multivariate Vector Slicing & Fubini's Theorem**: Higher-dimensional volumes defined via double and triple iterated integrals ($\iint_R f(x, y)\,dA$) are formally developed in Multivariable Calculus.
* **Non-Smooth Fractured Boundaries**: Arc length of non-rectifiable curves (such as the Koch snowflake or Brownian paths) requires Hausdorff dimension and measure theory, which exceeds Riemann integration.
* **Fluid Dynamics & Hydrostatic Force Vectors**: Generalized 2D/3D pressure tensors and Navier-Stokes boundary conditions are omitted in favor of standard 1D scalar hydrostatic integrals.
