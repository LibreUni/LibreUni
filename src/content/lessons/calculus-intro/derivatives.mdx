# Derivatives: Rates of Change

A particle's position along a line is modeled as $s(t)=\sqrt{t}$ metres after $t$ seconds. For $h\ne0$ with $4+h\ge0$, its average velocity between $t=4$ and $t=4+h$ is

$$
\frac{s(4+h)-s(4)}{h}=\frac{\sqrt{4+h}-2}{h}.
$$

At $h=0$ this expression becomes $0/0$, so substituting zero does not give an instantaneous velocity. Instead, rationalizing for $h\ne0$ gives $1/(\sqrt{4+h}+2)$. This approaches $1/4$ as $h$ approaches zero from either side: the limiting rate is $1/4$ m/s. The derivative formalizes this limiting process and, geometrically, gives the slope of a tangent line.

## Differentiability and the Derivative

We formally define the derivative of a function at a point as a limit of the difference quotient.

<MathStatement id="derivative-definition" kind="definition" title="The Derivative" number="1">

Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on an open interval $I$ containing $c$. The function $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $c$ if the limit

$$f'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h}$$

exists. If this limit exists, we denote it by $f'(c)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}(c)$.

</MathStatement>

Differentiability is a stronger condition than continuity. A function can be continuous but not differentiable, but the converse is always true.

<MathStatement id="diff-implies-cont" kind="theorem" title="Differentiability Implies Continuity" number="1" proofId="diff-implies-cont-proof">

If $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then $f$ is continuous at $c$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="diff-implies-cont-proof" for="diff-implies-cont">

To prove $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, it suffices to show that $\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = 0$. For $x \neq c$, we can write:

$$f(x) - f(c) = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \cdot (x - c).$$

Taking the limit as $x \to c$:

$$\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = \lim_{x \to c} \left( \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \cdot (x - c) \right).$$

Since $f$ is differentiable at $c$, the limit of the first term is $f'(c)$. Since $\lim_{x \to c} (x - c) = 0$, we have:

$$\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = f'(c) \cdot 0 = 0.$$

Therefore, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, and $f$ is continuous at $c$.

</MathProof>

A classic counterexample of a function that is continuous but not differentiable is $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$. The difference quotient as $h \to 0^+$ is $1$, but as $h \to 0^-$ is $-1$, so the limit does not exist.

**Worked trace: derivative from the definition via rationalization.** Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$. Find $f'(4)$ using the limit definition.
1. Form difference quotient: $\frac{\sqrt{4+h} - \sqrt{4}}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{4+h} - 2}{h}$.
2. Multiply numerator and denominator by conjugate $(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)$:
   $$
   \frac{(\sqrt{4+h} - 2)(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)} = \frac{(4+h) - 4}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)} = \frac{h}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)}.
   $$
3. Cancel $h \neq 0$:
   $$
   \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+h} + 2}.
   $$
4. Evaluate limit as $h \to 0$:
   $$
   f'(4) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+h} + 2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4} + 2} = \frac{1}{4}.
   $$

The calculation confirms the particle's instantaneous velocity at $t=4$: $1/4$ m/s. Cancel the factor $h$ only for $h\ne0$ before taking the limit; the quotient is not defined at $h=0$.

**Worked trace: a reciprocal function and a domain boundary.** Let $g(x)=1/x$ for $x\ne 0$. At $x=2$,

$$
g'(2)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{1}{2+h}-\frac{1}{2}}{h}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{2-(2+h)}{2(2+h)}}{h}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-h}{2h(2+h)}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-1}{2(2+h)}=-\frac14.
$$

Two features of this trace generalize. First, the same cancellation pattern appears whenever the numerator has a factor $h$; the algebra exposes the removable discontinuity rather than hiding it. Second, the computation is valid only at points where $g$ is defined on a two-sided neighbourhood. At $x=0$ the function is undefined, and no difference quotient exists, so “$g'(0)$” is not a number to be found—it is a question with no domain.

**Worked trace: a continuous function that fails differentiability.** Let

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}x\sin(1/x), & x\ne 0,\\ 0, & x=0.\end{cases}
$$

Continuity at $0$ follows from $|x\sin(1/x)|\le |x|$. The difference quotient is

$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\sin(1/h).
$$

As $h\to0$, $\sin(1/h)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ and has no limit. The function is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there. The counterexample shows that the converse of Theorem 1 is false and that a derivative can fail to exist even when the graph has no jump or corner in the usual pictorial sense.

To compute derivatives efficiently, we establish algebraic rules derived from the definition.

<MathStatement id="differentiation-rules" kind="theorem" title="Differentiation Rules" number="2" proofId="product-rule-proof">

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable at $x$.
1. **Sum Rule**: $(f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)$.
2. **Product Rule**: $(fg)'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$.
3. **Quotient Rule**: If $g(x) \neq 0$, $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'(x) = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^2}$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="product-rule-proof" for="differentiation-rules">

We prove the Product Rule. Let $F(x) = f(x)g(x)$. Using the definition:

$$F'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}.$$

Subtract and add the term $f(x)g(x+h)$ in the numerator:

$$F'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x+h) + f(x)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \cdot g(x+h) + f(x) \cdot \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h} \right).$$

Since $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, their individual limits exist. Since $g$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $\lim_{h \to 0} g(x+h) = g(x)$. Applying limit laws:

$$F'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).$$

</MathProof>

### The Chain Rule

The Chain Rule allows us to differentiate composite functions. It is the mathematical engine behind backpropagation in deep neural networks.

<MathStatement id="chain-rule" kind="theorem" title="The Chain Rule" number="3">

If $g$ is differentiable at $x$, and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composite function $y = (f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$, and:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x).$$

</MathStatement>

## Linear Approximation

The derivative provides the best linear approximation of a function near a point. Geometrically, this is the tangent line equation.

$$f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).$$

The tangent line is the limit of secant lines as the second point approaches $(a,f(a))$:

Let us visualize how the linear approximation matches $f(x) = \sin(x)$ around $a = 1.0$, and verify the error increases as we move away from $a$.

## Implicit Differentiation

When variables are defined implicitly (e.g., $x^2 + y^2 = 1$), we differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, applying the Chain Rule to $y$ (treating $y$ locally as a differentiable function of $x$).

For the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, differentiating yields:

$$2x + 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} \quad (y \neq 0).$$

> **Example.** On the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at $(3, 4)$, the slope is $\frac{dy}{dx}\big|_{(3,4)} = -\frac{3}{4}$, giving the tangent line $y - 4 = -\frac{3}{4}(x - 3)$, or $3x + 4y = 25$. At the equator points $(\pm 5, 0)$, the denominator $y = 0$ vanishes: the tangent line is vertical ($x = \pm 5$), and $y$ cannot be written as a differentiable function of $x$ on any neighborhood containing $(\pm 5, 0)$.

<details>
<summary>Worked trace: Folium of Descartes ($x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$ at $(3, 3)$)</summary>

1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, using the Product Rule on $6xy$:
   $$3x^2 + 3y^2 \frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x \frac{dy}{dx}.$$
2. Collect $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms:
   $$(3y^2 - 6x)\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y - 3x^2 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}.$$
3. Evaluate at $(3, 3)$:
   $$\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{(3,3)} = \frac{2(3) - 3^2}{3^2 - 2(3)} = \frac{6 - 9}{9 - 6} = -1.$$

</details>

## Deliberate Boundary and Deliberate Omissions

> **Remark.** Single-variable differentiation treats the derivative as a scalar slope $f'(c) \in \mathbb{R}$; in higher dimensions, the derivative is the unique linear map $Df(c): \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfying $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\|f(c+h) - f(c) - Df(c)h\|}{\|h\|} = 0$.

This lesson strictly bounds single-variable differentiation:
1. **Partial and directional derivatives** for functions of multiple variables ($f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$) are deferred to Multivariable Calculus.
2. **Weierstrass nowhere-differentiable continuous functions** and Brownian motion fractal paths are noted as boundaries but not constructed in full; full construction belongs to Real Analysis.
3. **Fréchet and Gâteaux derivatives** on infinite-dimensional Banach spaces are omitted.
4. **Differentiation of complex functions** (Cauchy-Riemann equations, holomorphic mappings) is reserved for Complex Analysis.
