A particle's position along a line is modeled as $s(t)=\sqrt{t}$ metres after $t$ seconds. For $h\ne0$ with $4+h\ge0$, its average velocity between $t=4$ and $t=4+h$ is

$$
\frac{s(4+h)-s(4)}{h}=\frac{\sqrt{4+h}-2}{h}.
$$

At $h=0$ this expression becomes $0/0$, so substituting zero does not give an instantaneous velocity. Instead, rationalizing for $h\ne0$ gives $1/(\sqrt{4+h}+2)$. This approaches $1/4$ as $h$ approaches zero from either side: the limiting rate is $1/4$ m/s. The derivative formalizes this limiting process and, geometrically, gives the slope of a tangent line.


## Differentiability and the Derivative

We formally define the derivative of a function at a point as a limit (<Ref id="limit-definition" />) of the difference quotient.

<MathStatement id="derivative-definition" kind="definition" title="The Derivative">

Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on an open interval $I$ containing $c$. The function $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $c$ if the limit

$$f'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h}$$

exists. If this limit exists, we denote it by $f'(c)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}(c)$.

</MathStatement>

<Figure id="fig-secants-to-tangent" />

Differentiability is a stronger condition than continuity (<Ref id="continuity-definition" />). A function can be continuous but not differentiable, but the converse is always true.

<MathStatement id="diff-implies-cont" kind="theorem" title="Differentiability Implies Continuity" proofId="diff-implies-cont-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "derivative-definition" }]}>

If $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then $f$ is continuous at $c$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="diff-implies-cont-proof" for="diff-implies-cont">

To prove $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, it suffices to show that $\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = 0$. For $x \neq c$, we can write:

$$f(x) - f(c) = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \cdot (x - c).$$

Taking the limit as $x \to c$:

$$\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = \lim_{x \to c} \left( \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \cdot (x - c) \right).$$

Since $f$ is differentiable at $c$, the limit of the first term is $f'(c)$. Since $\lim_{x \to c} (x - c) = 0$, we have:

$$\lim_{x \to c} (f(x) - f(c)) = f'(c) \cdot 0 = 0.$$

Therefore, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, and $f$ is continuous at $c$.

</MathProof>

A classic counterexample of a function that is continuous but not differentiable is $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$. The difference quotient as $h \to 0^+$ is $1$, but as $h \to 0^-$ is $-1$, so the limit does not exist.

<Figure id="fig-abs-corner" />

**Worked trace: derivative from the definition via rationalization.** Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$. Find $f'(4)$ using the limit definition.
1. Form difference quotient: $\frac{\sqrt{4+h} - \sqrt{4}}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{4+h} - 2}{h}$.
2. Multiply numerator and denominator by conjugate $(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)$:
   $$
   \frac{(\sqrt{4+h} - 2)(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)} = \frac{(4+h) - 4}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)} = \frac{h}{h(\sqrt{4+h} + 2)}.
   $$
3. Cancel $h \neq 0$:
   $$
   \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+h} + 2}.
   $$
4. Evaluate limit as $h \to 0$:
   $$
   f'(4) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+h} + 2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4} + 2} = \frac{1}{4}.
   $$

The calculation confirms the particle's instantaneous velocity at $t=4$: $1/4$ m/s. Cancel the factor $h$ only for $h\ne0$ before taking the limit; the quotient is not defined at $h=0$.

**Worked trace: a reciprocal function and a domain boundary.** Let $g(x)=1/x$ for $x\ne 0$. At $x=2$,

$$
g'(2)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{1}{2+h}-\frac{1}{2}}{h}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{2-(2+h)}{2(2+h)}}{h}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-h}{2h(2+h)}
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-1}{2(2+h)}=-\frac14.
$$

Two features of this trace generalize. First, the same cancellation pattern appears whenever the numerator has a factor $h$; the algebra exposes the removable discontinuity rather than hiding it. Second, the computation is valid only at points where $g$ is defined on a two-sided neighbourhood. At $x=0$ the function is undefined, and no difference quotient exists, so “$g'(0)$” is not a number to be found—it is a question with no domain.

**Worked trace: a continuous function that fails differentiability.** Let

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}x\sin(1/x), & x\ne 0,\\ 0, & x=0.\end{cases}
$$

Continuity at $0$ follows from $|x\sin(1/x)|\le |x|$. The difference quotient is

$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\sin(1/h).
$$

As $h\to0$, $\sin(1/h)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ and has no limit. The function is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there. The counterexample shows that the converse of <Ref id="diff-implies-cont" /> is false and that a derivative can fail to exist even when the graph has no jump or corner in the usual pictorial sense.

To compute derivatives efficiently, we establish algebraic rules derived from the definition.

<MathStatement id="differentiation-rules" kind="theorem" title="Differentiation Rules" proofId="product-rule-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "derivative-definition" }]}>

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable at $x$.
1. **Sum Rule**: $(f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)$.
2. **Product Rule**: $(fg)'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$.
3. **Quotient Rule**: If $g(x) \neq 0$, $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'(x) = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^2}$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="product-rule-proof" for="differentiation-rules">

We prove the Product Rule. Let $F(x) = f(x)g(x)$. Using the definition:

$$F'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}.$$

Subtract and add the term $f(x)g(x+h)$ in the numerator:

$$F'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x+h) + f(x)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \cdot g(x+h) + f(x) \cdot \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h} \right).$$

Since $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, their individual limits exist. Since $g$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $\lim_{h \to 0} g(x+h) = g(x)$. Applying limit laws:

$$F'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).$$

</MathProof>

### The Chain Rule

The Chain Rule allows us to differentiate composite functions. It is the mathematical engine behind backpropagation in deep neural networks.

<MathStatement id="chain-rule" kind="theorem" title="The Chain Rule" dependsOn={[{ id: "derivative-definition" }]}>

If $g$ is differentiable at $x$, and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composite function $y = (f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$, and:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x).$$

</MathStatement>


## Linear Approximation

The derivative provides the best linear approximation of a function near a point. Geometrically, this is the tangent line equation.

$$f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).$$

> **Remark.** Single-variable differentiation treats the derivative as a scalar slope $f'(c) \in \mathbb{R}$; in higher dimensions, the derivative is the unique linear map $Df(c): \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfying $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\|f(c+h) - f(c) - Df(c)h\|}{\|h\|} = 0$.

For example, linearising $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $a = 4$ gives $L(x) = 2 + \frac{1}{4}(x - 4)$, which closely tracks $\sqrt{x}$ near $x = 4$ while gradually diverging further away:

<Figure id="fig-sqrt-linearisation" />

## Implicit Differentiation

When variables are defined implicitly (e.g., $x^2 + y^2 = 1$), we differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, applying the Chain Rule to $y$ (treating $y$ locally as a differentiable function of $x$).

For the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, differentiating yields:

$$2x + 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} \quad (y \neq 0).$$

> **Example.** On the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at $(3, 4)$, the slope is $\frac{dy}{dx}\big|_{(3,4)} = -\frac{3}{4}$, giving the tangent line $y - 4 = -\frac{3}{4}(x - 3)$, or $3x + 4y = 25$. At the equator points $(\pm 5, 0)$, the denominator $y = 0$ vanishes: the tangent line is vertical ($x = \pm 5$), and $y$ cannot be written as a differentiable function of $x$ on any neighborhood containing $(\pm 5, 0)$.

<ExerciseSolution title="Worked trace: Folium of Descartes ($x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$ at $(3, 3)$)">

1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, using the Product Rule on $6xy$:
   $$3x^2 + 3y^2 \frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x \frac{dy}{dx}.$$
2. Collect $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms:
   $$(3y^2 - 6x)\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y - 3x^2 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}.$$
3. Evaluate at $(3, 3)$:
   $$\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{(3,3)} = \frac{2(3) - 3^2}{3^2 - 2(3)} = \frac{6 - 9}{9 - 6} = -1.$$

</ExerciseSolution>

<Exercise id="ex-deriv-first-principles" type="verify" level="application" title="Derivative from First Principles">

Using <Ref id="derivative-definition" />, compute the derivative of $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ at an arbitrary point $x > 0$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Form the difference quotient $\frac{1/\sqrt{x+h} - 1/\sqrt{x}}{h}$, find a common denominator, and rationalize the numerator.">

1. Form the difference quotient:
   $$\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{x+h}}{h \sqrt{x}\sqrt{x+h}}.$$
2. Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})$:
   $$\frac{(\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{x+h})(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})}{h \sqrt{x}\sqrt{x+h}(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})} = \frac{x - (x+h)}{h \sqrt{x}\sqrt{x+h}(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})} = \frac{-h}{h \sqrt{x}\sqrt{x+h}(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})}.$$
3. Cancel $h \ne 0$:
   $$\frac{-1}{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{x+h}(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x+h})}.$$
4. Take the limit as $h \to 0$ using limit laws:
   $$f'(x) = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{x}\sqrt{x}(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x})} = \frac{-1}{x(2\sqrt{x})} = -\frac{1}{2x^{3/2}}.$$

**Rubric.** Requires: (a) explicit difference quotient; (b) algebraic conjugate rationalization; (c) valid cancellation of $h$ for $h \ne 0$; (d) correct final algebraic term $-1/(2x^{3/2})$.

</ExerciseSolution>

<Exercise id="ex-diff-cont-counterexample" type="negate" level="recall" title="Continuity Without Differentiability">

Show that $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $x = 0$ by <Ref id="diff-implies-cont" />'s converse, but not differentiable at $x = 0$ by <Ref id="derivative-definition" />.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Evaluate the left-hand and right-hand limits of the difference quotient $(f(0+h) - f(0))/h$.">

<Figure id="fig-abs-corner" size="thumb" collapsed summary="Show corner diagram" />

**Continuity:**
$f(0) = 0$. Since $|f(x) - f(0)| = ||x| - 0| = |x| < \epsilon$ whenever $|x - 0| < \delta = \epsilon$, $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = f(0) = 0$. Thus $f$ is continuous at $0$.

**Non-Differentiability:**
The difference quotient at $c = 0$ is:
$$\frac{f(0+h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \frac{|h|}{h}.$$
As $h \to 0^+$, $|h| = h$, so $\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1$.
As $h \to 0^-$, $|h| = -h$, so $\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1$.
Since the one-sided limits disagree ($1 \ne -1$), the two-sided limit does not exist. Hence $f$ is not differentiable at $0$. This confirms the converse of <Ref id="diff-implies-cont" /> is false.

**Rubric.** Requires computing both one-sided difference quotient limits and concluding divergence.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-product-quotient-application" type="construct" level="application" title="Product and Quotient Rules on Algebraic Forms">

Using <Ref id="differentiation-rules" />, find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = \frac{x^2 \sin x}{x^2 + 1}$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Apply the Quotient Rule with numerator $u(x) = x^2 \sin x$ (which requires the Product Rule) and denominator $v(x) = x^2 + 1$.">

1. Let $u(x) = x^2 \sin x$. By the Product Rule:
   $$u'(x) = (x^2)' \sin x + x^2 (\sin x)' = 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x.$$
2. Let $v(x) = x^2 + 1$. Its derivative is $v'(x) = 2x$.
3. Applying the Quotient Rule:
   $$f'(x) = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{v(x)^2} = \frac{(2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x)(x^2 + 1) - (x^2 \sin x)(2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2}.$$
4. Expanding the numerator:
   $$(2x^3 \sin x + 2x \sin x + x^4 \cos x + x^2 \cos x) - 2x^3 \sin x = x^2(x^2 + 1)\cos x + 2x \sin x.$$
   Thus:
   $$f'(x) = \frac{x(x(x^2+1)\cos x + 2\sin x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2}.$$

**Rubric.** Full credit for correct composition of product and quotient rules and simplification.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-linear-approx-tangent" type="construct" level="application" title="Linear Approximation Error Bound">

Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$. Find the linear approximation $L(x)$ of $f$ at $a = 25$. Use $L(x)$ to estimate $\sqrt{26}$, and show without a calculator that the error is less than $1/500$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Recall $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$. For $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $f'(25) = 1/(2\sqrt{25}) = 1/10$.">

<Figure id="fig-sqrt-linearisation" size="thumb" collapsed summary="Show linearisation diagram" />

1. At $a = 25$: $f(25) = 5$, and $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, so $f'(25) = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1$.
2. The linear approximation is:
   $$L(x) = 5 + \frac{1}{10}(x - 25).$$
3. At $x = 26$:
   $$L(26) = 5 + \frac{1}{10}(26 - 25) = 5 + 0.1 = 5.1.$$
4. **Error analysis:**
   Squaring $5.1$ gives $(5.1)^2 = 26.01$.
   Since $26 < 26.01$, $\sqrt{26} < 5.1$.
   Also $(5.099)^2 = (5.1 - 0.001)^2 = 26.01 - 0.0102 + 0.000001 = 25.999801 < 26$.
   Thus $5.099 < \sqrt{26} < 5.100$, confirming $|\sqrt{26} - 5.1| < 0.001 < \frac{1}{500} = 0.002$.

**Rubric.** Requires the explicit tangent line equation, the estimate $5.1$, and the rigorous square comparison verifying the error bound.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-implicit-tangent" type="construct" level="application" title="Implicit Differentiation and Normal Slopes">

Consider the Folium of Descartes $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$. Find the equations of all horizontal and vertical tangent lines on the curve where $x \ne 0$ and $y \ne 0$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="From the worked trace, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}$. Horizontal tangents have numerator $= 0$; vertical tangents have denominator $= 0$.">

1. **Horizontal Tangents:**
   $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies 2y - x^2 = 0 \implies y = \frac{x^2}{2}$.
   Substitute into $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$:
   $$x^3 + \left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right)^3 = 6x\left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right) \implies x^3 + \frac{x^6}{8} = 3x^3 \implies \frac{x^6}{8} = 2x^3.$$
   Since $x \ne 0$, divide by $x^3$:
   $$x^3 = 16 \implies x = 2^{4/3} = 2\sqrt[3]{2}.$$
   Then $y = \frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{2^{8/3}}{2} = 2^{5/3} = 2\sqrt[3]{4} = 4^{2/3}$.
   The horizontal tangent line is $y = 2\sqrt[3]{4}$.

2. **Vertical Tangents:**
   By symmetry (swapping $x$ and $y$ leaves $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$ unchanged), the denominator vanishes when $y^2 - 2x = 0$, giving the vertical tangent at $x = 2\sqrt[3]{4}$.

**Rubric.** Requires setting numerator/denominator to zero, solving with the curve equation, and identifying the coordinates.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-inverse-function-differentiation" type="generalise" level="synthesis" title="Derivation of the Inverse Function Rule">

Let $f$ be strictly monotonic and differentiable on an interval $I$, with $f'(x) \ne 0$. Let $g = f^{-1}$ be its inverse.
Use <Ref id="chain-rule" /> on the identity $f(g(y)) = y$ to prove that $g'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(g(y))}$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Differentiate both sides of $f(g(y)) = y$ with respect to $y$, assuming $g$ is differentiable.">

1. The inverse function identity states:
   $$f(g(y)) = y \quad \text{for all } y \text{ in the range of } f.$$
2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $y$, applying the Chain Rule (<Ref id="chain-rule" />) to the composite left-hand side:
   $$\frac{d}{dy}[f(g(y))] = \frac{d}{dy}[y].$$
3. By <Ref id="chain-rule" />:
   $$f'(g(y)) \cdot g'(y) = 1.$$
4. Since $f'(x) \ne 0$ for all $x$, $f'(g(y)) \ne 0$. Dividing gives:
   $$g'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(g(y))}.$$
   Equivalently, setting $y = f(x)$ so $g(y) = x$:
   $$(f^{-1})'(f(x)) = \frac{1}{f'(x)}.$$

**Rubric.** Full credit for correct application of chain rule to identity and explicitly noting $f'(g(y)) \ne 0$.

</ExerciseSolution>
