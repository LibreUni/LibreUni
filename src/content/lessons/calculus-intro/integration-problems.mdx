<Problem
  id="prob-darboux-step-function"
  title="Integrability of Step Functions"
  type="prove"
  level="application"
  covers={["integration"]}
>
  Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a step function; that is, there exists a partition $P^* = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_k\}$ of $[a, b]$ such that on each open subinterval $(x_{i-1}, x_i)$, $f(x) = c_i$ for some constant $c_i \in \mathbb{R}$. At the partition points $x_i$, $f(x_i)$ may take arbitrary values in $\mathbb{R}$.

  Prove from the partition integrability criterion (<Ref id="riemann-integrable" />) that $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$, and that its integral equals $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i (x_i - x_{i-1})$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Refine $P^*$ by placing narrow subintervals of width $\delta$ around each boundary point $x_i$ where $f$ differs from the constants $c_i$. Since there are only $k+1$ boundary points and $f$ is bounded, the total variation contributed by the boundary spikes can be bounded by $2(k+1)M\delta$.
    </div>

    Let $M = \sup_{x \in [a, b]} |f(x)|$. Since $f$ takes only finitely many values, $M < \infty$.

    Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $\delta > 0$ such that $2(k+1) \cdot 2M \cdot \delta < \varepsilon$. Construct a refined partition $P$ by placing symmetric intervals $[x_i - \delta, x_i + \delta]$ around each interior partition point $x_i$ (and $[a, a+\delta]$, $[b-\delta, b]$ at endpoints).

    On subintervals where $f$ is constant $c_i$, the supremum equals the infimum, so $M_j - m_j = 0$. The only subintervals contributing to $U(f, P) - L(f, P)$ are the intervals enclosing the partition points $x_i$. There are at most $2k$ such intervals, each of width at most $2\delta$, on which $M_j - m_j \le 2M$.

    Therefore:
    $$U(f, P) - L(f, P) \le \sum_{\text{boundary } j} (M_j - m_j) \Delta x_j \le 2M \cdot 2(k+1)\delta < \varepsilon.$$
    By <Ref id="riemann-integrable" />, $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$. Furthermore, as $\delta \to 0$, the sum of rectangles on the constant intervals converges to $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i (x_i - x_{i-1})$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-dirichlet-non-integrable"
  title="Upper and Lower Darboux Integrals of a Linearly Modulated Dirichlet Function"
  type="prove"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["integration"]}
>
  Define $f: [0, 2] \to \mathbb{R}$ by:
  $$f(x) = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 2] \\ -x & \text{if } x \in (\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}) \cap [0, 2]. \end{cases}$$

  Compute the exact upper Darboux integral $\overline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx$ and lower Darboux integral $\underline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx$, and deduce whether $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 2]$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      On each subinterval $[x_{i-1}, x_i] \subseteq [0, 2]$, use the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ to show that $M_i = x_i$ and $m_i = -x_i$. Compare with the upper and lower sums of $g(x) = x$.
    </div>

    Let $P = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ be any partition of $[0, 2]$. On each subinterval $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ with $0 \le x_{i-1} < x_i \le 2$:
    1. By density of $\mathbb{Q}$, rationals in $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ come arbitrarily close to the right endpoint $x_i$, where $f(r) = r \le x_i$. Since $f(x) \le x \le x_i$ for all $x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]$, we have $M_i = \sup_{x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]} f(x) = x_i$.
    2. By density of $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, irrationals in $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ come arbitrarily close to $x_i$, where $f(s) = -s \ge -x_i$. Hence $m_i = \inf_{x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]} f(x) = -x_i$.

    Therefore, for every partition $P$:
    $$U(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \Delta x_i = U(g, P), \qquad L(f, P) = -\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \Delta x_i = -U(g, P),$$
    where $g(x) = x$ is continuous and integrable on $[0, 2]$ with $\int_0^2 x\,dx = \frac{2^2}{2} = 2$.
    Taking the infimum of $U(f, P)$ and supremum of $L(f, P)$ over all partitions $P$:
    $$\overline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx = \inf_P U(g, P) = \int_0^2 x\,dx = 2,$$
    $$\underline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx = \sup_P \bigl(-U(g, P)\bigr) = -\inf_P U(g, P) = -2.$$
    Because $\underline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx = -2 \neq 2 = \overline{\int_0^2} f(x)\,dx$, $f$ is not Riemann integrable on $[0, 2]$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-riemann-oscillation-criterion"
  title="The Oscillation Criterion for Integrability"
  type="prove"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["integration"]}
>
  For a bounded function $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ and any subinterval $J \subseteq [a, b]$, define the oscillation of $f$ on $J$ by $\omega(f, J) = \sup_{x \in J} f(x) - \inf_{x \in J} f(x) = \sup_{x, y \in J} |f(x) - f(y)|$.

  Prove that $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a partition $P = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ such that:
  $$\sum_{i=1}^n \omega(f, [x_{i-1}, x_i]) \Delta x_i < \varepsilon.$$

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Rewrite the definition of $U(f, P) - L(f, P)$ by grouping terms with the common factor $\Delta x_i$.
    </div>

    By definition of the upper and lower Darboux sums:
    $$U(f, P) - L(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i \Delta x_i - \sum_{i=1}^n m_i \Delta x_i = \sum_{i=1}^n (M_i - m_i) \Delta x_i.$$
    Notice that $M_i - m_i = \sup_{x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]} f(x) - \inf_{x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i]} f(x) = \omega(f, [x_{i-1}, x_i])$.

    Therefore, the sum $\sum_{i=1}^n \omega(f, [x_{i-1}, x_i]) \Delta x_i$ is identically equal to $U(f, P) - L(f, P)$.
    The claim follows immediately from the Darboux integrability criterion (<Ref id="riemann-integrable" />), which states that $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a partition $P$ satisfying $U(f, P) - L(f, P) < \varepsilon$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-thomae-integrable"
  title="Integrability of Thomae's Ruler Function"
  type="prove"
  level="synthesis"
  challenge={true}
  covers={["integration"]}
>
  Thomae's function $T: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is defined by:
  $$T(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{q} & \text{if } x = \frac{p}{q} \in \mathbb{Q} \cap (0, 1] \text{ in lowest terms } (p, q \in \mathbb{N}, \gcd(p, q)=1) \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x \in [0, 1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}. \end{cases}$$

  Prove that $T$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 1]$ and that $\int_0^1 T(x)\,dx = 0$, despite being discontinuous at every rational number in $[0, 1]$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Notice that for any $N \in \mathbb{N}$, there are only finitely many fractions in $[0, 1]$ with denominator $q \le N$. Everywhere else, $0 \le T(x) < 1/N$.
    </div>

    First, for every partition $P$, every subinterval contains irrationals where $T(x) = 0$. Since $T(x) \ge 0$, $m_i = \inf_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} T(x) = 0$, so $L(T, P) = 0$ for all $P$. Thus, $L(T) = \sup_P L(T, P) = 0$.

    To prove integrability, we must show that $\inf_P U(T, P) = 0$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$.
    Choose an integer $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $\frac{1}{N} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.
    The set of points $S = \{x \in [0, 1] : T(x) \ge 1/N\}$ consists solely of rationals with denominator $q \le N$. The cardinality $|S| = K$ is finite.

    Choose $\delta > 0$ such that $2 K \delta < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$. Construct a partition $P$ of $[0, 1]$ having maximum subinterval width $\Delta x_i < \delta$.
    Partition the index set $\{1, \dots, n\}$ into two disjoint sets:
    - $I_{\text{bad}}$: indices where $[x_{i-1}, x_i] \cap S \neq \emptyset$.
    - $I_{\text{good}}$: indices where $[x_{i-1}, x_i] \cap S = \emptyset$.

    Each point in $S$ can belong to at most 2 adjacent subintervals, so $|I_{\text{bad}}| \le 2K$. On these intervals, $M_i \le 1$.
    Thus:
    $$\sum_{i \in I_{\text{bad}}} M_i \Delta x_i \le 1 \cdot \sum_{i \in I_{\text{bad}}} \Delta x_i < 1 \cdot (2K\delta) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

    On good intervals, $T(x) < 1/N$ everywhere, so $M_i \le 1/N < \varepsilon/2$. Thus:
    $$\sum_{i \in I_{\text{good}}} M_i \Delta x_i \le \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \in I_{\text{good}}} \Delta x_i \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

    Combining both sums:
    $$U(T, P) = \sum_{i \in I_{\text{bad}}} M_i \Delta x_i + \sum_{i \in I_{\text{good}}} M_i \Delta x_i < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.$$
    Since $U(T, P) - L(T, P) < \varepsilon$, $T$ is Riemann integrable by <Ref id="riemann-integrable" />, and $\int_0^1 T(x)\,dx = 0$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-ftc-leibniz-variable-limits"
  title="Stationary Points of a Variable-Limit Gaussian Integral"
  type="construct"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["fundamental-theorem"]}
>
  Define the function $G: [0, \pi] \to \mathbb{R}$ by:
  $$G(x) = \int_{\cos x}^{\sin x} e^{-t^2}\,dt.$$

  1. Use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (<Ref id="ftc-part-1" />) and the Chain Rule (<Ref id="chain-rule" />) to compute $G'(x)$.
  2. Find all critical points $x \in (0, \pi)$ where $G'(x) = 0$, and determine the exact maximum value of $G(x)$ on $[0, \pi]$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Split the integral at $0$ as $G(x) = \int_0^{\sin x} e^{-t^2}\,dt - \int_0^{\cos x} e^{-t^2}\,dt$, differentiate using the Chain Rule, and observe when $\cos x \cdot e^{-\sin^2 x} + \sin x \cdot e^{-\cos^2 x} = 0$ or when the upper and lower limits coincide.
    </div>

    **1. Derivative via FTC Part 1 and Chain Rule:**
    Let $F(u) = \int_0^u e^{-t^2}\,dt$. Since $f(t) = e^{-t^2}$ is continuous everywhere, <Ref id="ftc-part-1" /> gives $F'(u) = e^{-u^2}$.
    Writing $G(x) = F(\sin x) - F(\cos x)$ and applying <Ref id="chain-rule" />:
    $$G'(x) = F'(\sin x)\cos x - F'(\cos x)(-\sin x) = \cos(x)\,e^{-\sin^2 x} + \sin(x)\,e^{-\cos^2 x}.$$

    **2. Critical Points on $(0, \pi)$:**
    On $(0, \pi/2]$, both $\sin x > 0$ and $\cos x \ge 0$, so $G'(x) > 0$.
    On $(\pi/2, \pi)$, $\sin x > 0$ and $\cos x < 0$. Notice that at $x = \frac{3\pi}{4}$, we have $\sin(3\pi/4) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $\cos(3\pi/4) = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, so $\sin^2 x = \cos^2 x = \frac{1}{2}$ and:
    $$G'\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right) = \left(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)e^{-1/2} = 0.$$
    For $x \in (0, 3\pi/4)$, $G'(x) > 0$; for $x \in (3\pi/4, \pi)$, $G'(x) < 0$. Thus $x = \frac{3\pi}{4}$ is the unique critical point and global maximum on $[0, \pi]$, with value $G(3\pi/4) = \int_{-\sqrt{2}/2}^{\sqrt{2}/2} e^{-t^2}\,dt$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-ftc-discontinuous-integrand"
  title="Accumulation of the Floor Step Function"
  type="prove"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["integration", "fundamental-theorem"]}
>
  Let $f: [0, 3] \to \mathbb{R}$ be the floor function $f(t) = \lfloor t \rfloor$, and define the accumulation function $F(x) = \int_0^x \lfloor t \rfloor\,dt$ for $x \in [0, 3]$.

  1. Derive an explicit piecewise formula for $F(x)$ on $[0, 3]$.
  2. Prove that $F$ is continuous on the entire interval $[0, 3]$, but fails to be differentiable at the interior jump points $x = 1$ and $x = 2$. Explain why this behavior is consistent with <Ref id="ftc-part-1" />.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      On $[0, 1)$, $\lfloor t \rfloor = 0$; on $[1, 2)$, $\lfloor t \rfloor = 1$; on $[2, 3)$, $\lfloor t \rfloor = 2$. Accumulate the area subinterval by subinterval and compare one-sided derivatives at $x = 1$ and $x = 2$.
    </div>

    **1. Explicit Piecewise Formula:**
    - For $x \in [0, 1]$: $F(x) = \int_0^x 0\,dt = 0$.
    - For $x \in (1, 2]$: $F(x) = \int_0^1 0\,dt + \int_1^x 1\,dt = x - 1$.
    - For $x \in (2, 3]$: $F(x) = 0 + \int_1^2 1\,dt + \int_2^x 2\,dt = 1 + 2(x - 2) = 2x - 3$.

    **2. Continuity and Non-Differentiability at Jump Points:**
    At $x = 1$: $\lim_{x \to 1^-} F(x) = 0 = \lim_{x \to 1^+} (x - 1) = F(1)$, and at $x = 2$: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} (x - 1) = 1 = \lim_{x \to 2^+} (2x - 3) = F(2)$. Thus $F$ is continuous on $[0, 3]$.
    However, at $x = 1$, the left-hand derivative is $F'_{-}(1) = 0$ while the right-hand derivative is $F'_{+}(1) = 1$. At $x = 2$, $F'_{-}(2) = 1$ while $F'_{+}(2) = 2$. Thus $F'(1)$ and $F'(2)$ do not exist.
    This is consistent with <Ref id="ftc-part-1" />: the accumulation function of any Riemann integrable function is continuous, while differentiability $F'(x) = f(x)$ is guaranteed at points $x$ where $f$ is continuous (here, on $(0, 1) \cup (1, 2) \cup (2, 3)$).
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-ftc-accumulation-extrema"
  title="Critical Analysis of an Accumulation Function"
  type="describe"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["fundamental-theorem"]}
>
  Let $F: [0, 4] \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined by $F(x) = \int_0^x \frac{t - 2}{t^2 + 1}\,dt$.

  1. Find all critical points of $F$ on $(0, 4)$ and classify each as a local minimum, local maximum, or inflection point.
  2. Determine the intervals on which $F$ is concave upward and concave downward.
  3. Identify the location of the absolute minimum of $F$ on $[0, 4]$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      By FTC Part 1, $F'(x) = \frac{x-2}{x^2+1}$. Find where $F'(x) = 0$ and check the sign of $F''(x)$.
    </div>

    **1. Critical Points:**
    The integrand $f(t) = \frac{t-2}{t^2+1}$ is continuous on $[0, 4]$. By <Ref id="ftc-part-1" />:
    $$F'(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x^2 + 1}.$$
    Setting $F'(x) = 0$ gives $x - 2 = 0 \implies x = 2$.
    For $x \in (0, 2)$, $F'(x) < 0$ ($F$ is strictly decreasing).
    For $x \in (2, 4)$, $F'(x) > 0$ ($F$ is strictly increasing).
    By the First Derivative Test, $x = 2$ is a strict local minimum.

    **2. Concavity and Inflection Points:**
    Differentiating $F'(x)$ using the quotient rule:
    $$F''(x) = \frac{1 \cdot (x^2 + 1) - (x - 2)(2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{x^2 + 1 - 2x^2 + 4x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{-x^2 + 4x + 1}{(x^2 + 1)^2}.$$
    Setting $F''(x) = 0$ yields $-x^2 + 4x + 1 = 0 \implies x = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 4}}{-2} = 2 \pm \sqrt{5}$.
    Since $2 + \sqrt{5} \approx 4.236 \notin [0, 4]$ and $2 - \sqrt{5} \approx -0.236 \notin [0, 4]$, the numerator $-x^2 + 4x + 1$ is strictly positive for all $x \in [0, 4]$ (at $x=0$, it is $+1$; at $x=2$, $+5$; at $x=4$, $+1$).
    Therefore, $F''(x) > 0$ for all $x \in [0, 4]$, meaning $F$ is concave upward on the entire interval $[0, 4]$.

    **3. Absolute Minimum:**
    Since $F$ decreases on $[0, 2]$ and increases on $[2, 4]$, the absolute minimum on $[0, 4]$ occurs uniquely at $x = 2$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-integral-mean-value-point"
  title="Locating the Mean Value Point for Integrals"
  type="verify"
  level="application"
  covers={["integration", "fundamental-theorem"]}
>
  Consider $f(x) = 3x^2 - 4x + 1$ on the interval $[0, 2]$.

  1. Calculate the exact average value $\mu = \frac{1}{2 - 0}\int_0^2 f(x)\,dx$.
  2. Find all values of $c \in (0, 2)$ that satisfy the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals (<Ref id="mvt-integrals" />), such that $f(c) = \mu$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Evaluate the integral using FTC Part 2 (<Ref id="ftc-part-2" />), then solve the quadratic equation $f(c) = \mu$.
    </div>

    **1. Definite Integral and Average Value:**
    An antiderivative of $f(x) = 3x^2 - 4x + 1$ is $F(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 + x$.
    By <Ref id="ftc-part-2" />:
    $$\int_0^2 (3x^2 - 4x + 1)\,dx = F(2) - F(0) = (8 - 8 + 2) - 0 = 2.$$
    The average value is:
    $$\mu = \frac{1}{2 - 0} \int_0^2 f(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1.$$

    **2. Mean Value Point:**
    By <Ref id="mvt-integrals" />, there exists $c \in (0, 2)$ such that $f(c) = 1$:
    $$3c^2 - 4c + 1 = 1 \iff 3c^2 - 4c = 0 \iff c(3c - 4) = 0.$$
    The solutions to the quadratic are $c = 0$ and $c = 4/3$.
    Since the theorem requires $c \in (0, 2)$ strictly in the open interval, the endpoint $c = 0$ is excluded.
    The unique valid mean value point is $c = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333 \in (0, 2)$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-area-between-cubic-line"
  title="Area Between Cubic and Linear Curves"
  type="construct"
  level="application"
  covers={["applications-integration"]}
>
  Compute the total area of the finite regions bounded between the cubic curve $y = x^3 - 3x$ and the line $y = x$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Find all intersection points by solving $x^3 - 3x = x$. Note that the curves intersect at three points, so the area must be split into two separate subintervals where the top and bottom curves alternate.
    </div>

    **1. Intersection Points:**
    Set $x^3 - 3x = x \iff x^3 - 4x = 0 \iff x(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0$.
    The intersection abscissae are $x = -2$, $x = 0$, and $x = 2$.

    **2. Relative Positions:**
    - On $[-2, 0]$: test $x = -1$. Cubic: $(-1)^3 - 3(-1) = 2$. Line: $-1$. Since $2 > -1$, $x^3 - 3x \ge x$.
    - On $[0, 2]$: test $x = 1$. Cubic: $1^3 - 3(1) = -2$. Line: $1$. Since $1 > -2$, $x \ge x^3 - 3x$.

    **3. Integration:**
    $$A = \int_{-2}^0 ((x^3 - 3x) - x)\,dx + \int_0^2 (x - (x^3 - 3x))\,dx.$$
    Notice that the integrand $g(x) = (x^3 - 3x) - x = x^3 - 4x$ is an odd function, so the areas of the two lobes are symmetric:
    $$A_1 = \int_{-2}^0 (x^3 - 4x)\,dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - 2x^2 \right]_{-2}^0 = 0 - (4 - 8) = 4.$$
    $$A_2 = \int_0^2 (4x - x^3)\,dx = \left[ 2x^2 - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^2 = (8 - 4) - 0 = 4.$$
    The total enclosed area is $A = A_1 + A_2 = 4 + 4 = 8$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-area-horizontal-slicing"
  title="Horizontal Versus Vertical Slicing"
  type="construct"
  level="transfer"
  covers={["applications-integration"]}
>
  Find the area enclosed between the two parabolas $x = y^2 - 4y$ and $x = 2y - y^2$.
  Set up and evaluate the integral with respect to $y$, and explain why horizontal slicing is computationally preferable to vertical slicing for this region.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Solve for the $y$-coordinates of intersection by equating the two formulas for $x$. Then integrate $(x_{\text{right}} - x_{\text{left}})\,dy$.
    </div>

    **1. Intersection Points:**
    Set $y^2 - 4y = 2y - y^2 \iff 2y^2 - 6y = 0 \iff 2y(y - 3) = 0$.
    The curves intersect at $y = 0$ and $y = 3$.

    **2. Orientation:**
    For $y \in (0, 3)$, test $y = 1$:
    $x_1 = 1^2 - 4(1) = -3$ (left curve).
    $x_2 = 2(1) - 1^2 = 1$ (right curve).
    Thus $x_{\text{right}} = 2y - y^2$ and $x_{\text{left}} = y^2 - 4y$.

    **3. Horizontal Slicing:**
    $$A = \int_0^3 (x_{\text{right}} - x_{\text{left}})\,dy = \int_0^3 ((2y - y^2) - (y^2 - 4y))\,dy = \int_0^3 (6y - 2y^2)\,dy.$$
    Integrating:
    $$A = \left[ 3y^2 - \frac{2}{3}y^3 \right]_0^3 = \left( 3(9) - \frac{2}{3}(27) \right) - 0 = 27 - 18 = 9.$$

    **4. Comparison with Vertical Slicing:**
    Solving $x = y^2 - 4y$ for $y$ yields $y = 2 \pm \sqrt{x + 4}$, and solving $x = 2y - y^2$ yields $y = 1 \pm \sqrt{1 - x}$.
    Integrating with respect to $x$ requires splitting the domain $[-4, 1]$ into three separate piecewise subintervals $[-4, -3]$, $[-3, 0]$, and $[0, 1]$, and integrating radical expressions on each. Horizontal slicing requires only a single, simple polynomial integral.
  </ExerciseSolution>
</Problem>

<Problem
  id="prob-synthesis-tangent-area"
  title="Parabolic Tangent Area Invariance"
  type="prove"
  level="synthesis"
  covers={["fundamental-theorem", "applications-integration"]}
>
  Let $f(x) = x^2$. For any positive constant $a > 0$, consider:
  - The parabola $y = x^2$.
  - The tangent line to the parabola at the point $(a, a^2)$.
  - The vertical $y$-axis ($x = 0$).

  Prove that the area of the region bounded by these three curves is always equal to $\frac{1}{3} a^3$, which is precisely one-third of the area under $y = x^2$ over $[0, a]$.

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Find the equation of the tangent line at $x=a$ using $f'(a) = 2a$. Then integrate the vertical difference $(x^2 - y_{\text{tangent}})\,dx$ from $x = 0$ to $x = a$.
    </div>

    **1. Tangent Line Equation:**
    The derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ is $f'(x) = 2x$, so the slope at $x = a$ is $m = 2a$.
    The tangent line equation in point-slope form is:
    $$y - a^2 = 2a(x - a) \iff y = 2ax - a^2.$$

    **2. Bounded Region:**
    On the interval $[0, a]$, the parabola lies above its tangent line:
    $$x^2 - (2ax - a^2) = x^2 - 2ax + a^2 = (x - a)^2 \ge 0.$$
    The region is bounded on the left by $x = 0$, on the right by $x = a$, from above by $y = x^2$, and from below by $y = 2ax - a^2$.

    **3. Area Integration:**
    Applying vertical slicing:
    $$A = \int_0^a (x^2 - (2ax - a^2))\,dx = \int_0^a (x - a)^2\,dx.$$
    Using the substitution $u = x - a$ with $du = dx$ (or expanding directly):
    $$A = \left[ \frac{(x - a)^3}{3} \right]_0^a = \frac{(a - a)^3}{3} - \frac{(0 - a)^3}{3} = 0 - \frac{-a^3}{3} = \frac{1}{3} a^3.$$

    Since $\int_0^a x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^a = \frac{1}{3} a^3$, the tangent region area $\frac{1}{3}a^3$ equals exactly one-third of the enclosing bounding rectangle $a \times a^2 = a^3$, and matches the area under the parabola.
  </ExerciseSolution>

>
  Consider $f(x) = x^3$ on the interval $[0, 1]$.
  For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, let $P_n$ be the uniform partition with $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = \frac{1}{n}$, with division points $x_i = \frac{i}{n}$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$.

  1. Using the cubic power-sum identity $\sum_{i=1}^k i^3 = \left[\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right]^2$, derive closed-form formulas in terms of $n$ for $L(f, P_n)$ and $U(f, P_n)$.
  2. Prove that $U(f, P_n) - L(f, P_n) = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
  3. Deduce $\int_0^1 x^3\,dx$ directly from <Ref id="riemann-integrable" />.

  <CodeExercise
    client:load
    title="Compute Upper and Lower Darboux Sums for f(x) = x^3"
    code={`def darboux_x3(n):
    # For strictly increasing f(x) = x^3 on [0, 1] with uniform partition of width 1/n:
    # Upper sum uses right endpoints x_i = i / n
    u_sum = sum(([!blank!]) * (1 / n) for i in range(1, n + 1))
    # Lower sum uses left endpoints x_{i-1} = (i - 1) / n
    l_sum = sum(((i - 1) / n) ** 3 * (1 / n) for i in range(1, n + 1))
    return (l_sum, u_sum, u_sum - l_sum)`}

    explanation="Since f(x) = x^3 is strictly increasing on [0, 1], its supremum on [(i-1)/n, i/n] occurs at the right endpoint x_i = i/n, giving M_i = (i / n) ** 3."

  <ExerciseSolution>
    <div slot="hint">
      Apply $\sum_{i=1}^k i^3 = \frac{k^2(k+1)^2}{4}$ with $k = n$ for the upper sum and $k = n - 1$ for the lower sum.
    </div>

    **1. Closed-Form Darboux Sums:**
    Since $f(x) = x^3$ is strictly increasing on $[0, 1]$, on each subinterval $[(i-1)/n, i/n]$ we have $m_i = \frac{(i-1)^3}{n^3}$ and $M_i = \frac{i^3}{n^3}$ with $\Delta x_i = \frac{1}{n}$.
    Using $\sum_{i=1}^k i^3 = \frac{k^2(k+1)^2}{4}$:
    $$U(f, P_n) = \frac{1}{n^4}\sum_{i=1}^n i^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4n^4} = \frac{(n+1)^2}{4n^2},$$
    $$L(f, P_n) = \frac{1}{n^4}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j^3 = \frac{(n-1)^2 n^2}{4n^4} = \frac{(n-1)^2}{4n^2}.$$

    **2. Darboux Gap Convergence:**
    $$U(f, P_n) - L(f, P_n) = \frac{(n+1)^2 - (n-1)^2}{4n^2} = \frac{4n}{4n^2} = \frac{1}{n} \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$
    By <Ref id="riemann-integrable" />, $f(x) = x^3$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 1]$.

    **3. Value of the Integral:**
    Taking $n \to \infty$:
    $$\lim_{n \to \infty} U(f, P_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n^2 + 2n + 1}{4n^2} = \frac{1}{4} = \lim_{n \to \infty} L(f, P_n).$$
    Therefore $\int_0^1 x^3\,dx = \frac{1}{4}$.
  </ExerciseSolution>
