<Problem id="ex-quotient-rule-proof" type="prove" level="synthesis" title="Algebra of Limits: Bounding the Denominator" challenge={true} covers={['limits']}>

Complete the proof of the Algebra of Limits (<Ref id="algebra-of-limits" />): assuming $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M \ne 0$, prove there is a $\delta_0 > 0$ such that $|g(x)| > |M|/2$ whenever $0 < |x - c| < \delta_0$; then use this to derive the quotient rule from the product and reciprocal rules.

<ExerciseSolution hint="First pin $g$ away from $0$ with $\epsilon = |M|/2$ in the limit definition; the preliminary bound then controls the quotient $|1/g(x) - 1/M|$.">

**Step 1 (pin away from zero).** Apply <Ref id="limit-definition" /> with $\epsilon = |M|/2$: there is $\delta_0 > 0$ such that $0 < |x-c| < \delta_0$ implies $|g(x) - M| < |M|/2$. Then by the reverse triangle inequality,

$$|g(x)| = |M + (g(x) - M)| \ge |M| - |g(x) - M| > |M| - \tfrac{|M|}{2} = \tfrac{|M|}{2} > 0.$$

**Step 2 (reciprocal).** For $0 < |x - c| < \delta_0$:

$$\left|\frac{1}{g(x)} - \frac{1}{M}\right| = \frac{|M - g(x)|}{|g(x)|\,|M|} < \frac{|g(x) - M|}{(|M|/2)\,|M|} = \frac{2}{|M|^2}\,|g(x) - M|.$$

Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta_1$ so that $|g(x) - M| < \epsilon\,|M|^2/2$ for $0 < |x - c| < \delta_1$, and set $\delta = \min(\delta_0, \delta_1)$. Then $|1/g(x) - 1/M| < \epsilon$ throughout, so $\lim_{x \to c} 1/g(x) = 1/M$.

**Step 3 (quotient).** The product rule of <Ref id="algebra-of-limits" /> applied to $f$ and $1/g$ gives $\lim f/g = L/M$. ∎

**Rubric.** Full credit requires all three steps: the preliminary pin $|g(x)| > |M|/2$ (with $\epsilon = |M|/2$ explicit), the reciprocal estimate with the $2/|M|^2$ constant appearing, and the final composition with the product rule.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-invented-quantifier-swap" type="invented-definition" level="transfer" title="Quantifier Perturbation: The 'Verconges' Definition" challenge={true} covers={['limits']}>

Suppose a mathematician proposes an altered definition of limit: a function $f$ is said to *verconge* to $L$ at $c$ if:
**There exists $\delta > 0$ such that for every $\epsilon > 0$**, for all $x$:
$$0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon.$$

Characterize all functions that verconge to $L$ at $c$. What does this reveal about why the order of quantifiers in <Ref id="limit-definition" /> matters?

<ExerciseSolution hint="If $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$ for every $\epsilon > 0$ on a fixed interval, what is the only non-negative number that can satisfy that?">

**Characterization:**
A function $f$ verconges to $L$ at $c$ **if and only if $f(x) = L$ identically on the entire punctured neighborhood $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \setminus \{c\}$**.

**Proof:**
Suppose $f$ verconges to $L$ at $c$, so there exists a fixed $\delta > 0$ such that for every $x \in (c - \delta, c + \delta) \setminus \{c\}$, the distance $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$ for *every* $\epsilon > 0$.
The only non-negative real number that is strictly less than every positive real number $\epsilon$ is $0$.
Therefore, for every $x$ in the punctured window, $|f(x) - L| = 0$, which implies $f(x) = L$.
Conversely, if $f(x) = L$ on $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \setminus \{c\}$, then $|f(x) - L| = 0 < \epsilon$ holds for all $\epsilon > 0$.

**Significance:**
In <Ref id="limit-definition" />, $\delta$ is chosen *after* $\epsilon$ ($\forall \epsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0$). This allows $\delta$ to shrink as the tolerance $\epsilon$ tightens, capturing *local sensitivity* and convergence. Swapping the quantifiers forces one static window $\delta$ to satisfy all tolerances at once, which destroys the entire theory of continuous change and restricts the concept to trivial locally constant functions.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="prob-eps-delta-derivative" type="prove" level="synthesis" title="Epsilon-Delta Derivative Existence and Lipschitz Bound" covers={['limits', 'derivatives']}>

Let $f(x) = x^3$.
1. Using <Ref id="limit-definition" />, give a rigorous $\epsilon$-$\delta$ proof that $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h} = 3x^2$ at an arbitrary fixed $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
2. Using <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" />, show that for all $x_1, x_2 \in [1, 3]$, $|x_1^3 - x_2^3| \le 27 |x_1 - x_2|$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Expand $(x+h)^3$, factor out $h$, and freeze the variable factor in the difference quotient with a preliminary bound $|h| \le 1$.">

**Part 1: $\epsilon$-$\delta$ proof of the derivative.**
For $h \ne 0$:
$$\frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h} = \frac{3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2.$$
The difference from the candidate limit $3x^2$ is:
$$\left|\left(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2\right) - 3x^2\right| = |3xh + h^2| = |h|\,|3x + h|.$$
Impose the preliminary bound $|h| \le 1$. Then by the triangle inequality, $|3x + h| \le 3|x| + |h| \le 3|x| + 1$.
Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose:
$$\delta = \min\left(1, \frac{\epsilon}{3|x| + 1}\right).$$
Whenever $0 < |h| < \delta$, we have $|h| \le 1$ and:
$$\left|\frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h} - 3x^2\right| \le |h|(3|x| + 1) < \frac{\epsilon}{3|x| + 1} (3|x| + 1) = \epsilon.$$
This establishes that $f'(x) = 3x^2$ directly from <Ref id="limit-definition" />.

**Part 2: Lipschitz bound via MVT.**
Let $x_1, x_2 \in [1, 3]$ with $x_1 < x_2$.
By <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" /> applied to $f(t) = t^3$ on $[x_1, x_2]$, there exists $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:
$$x_2^3 - x_1^3 = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) = 3c^2(x_2 - x_1).$$
Since $c \in (x_1, x_2) \subset [1, 3]$, $c \le 3$, so $3c^2 \le 3(3^2) = 27$.
Taking absolute values:
$$|x_2^3 - x_1^3| = 3c^2 |x_2 - x_1| \le 27 |x_2 - x_1|.$$

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-discontinuous-derivative" type="example-or-impossible" level="transfer" title="Differentiable Function with Discontinuous Derivative" challenge={true} covers={['derivatives']}>

Define $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$, and $f(0) = 0$.
Prove that $f$ is differentiable everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, but the derivative function $f'$ is discontinuous at $x = 0$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Use the limit definition directly at $x = 0$. For $x \ne 0$, use standard differentiation rules and inspect the limit of $f'(x)$ as $x \to 0$.">

**Differentiability at $0$:**
By <Ref id="derivative-definition" />:
$$f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h) - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h \sin(1/h).$$
Since $|\sin(1/h)| \le 1$, we have $|h \sin(1/h)| \le |h| \to 0$. By the Squeeze Theorem, $f'(0) = 0$. Thus $f$ is differentiable at $0$.

**Derivative for $x \ne 0$:**
By <Ref id="differentiation-rules" /> and <Ref id="chain-rule" />:
$$f'(x) = 2x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) + x^2 \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right) = 2x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right).$$

**Discontinuity of $f'$ at $0$:**
As $x \to 0$, $2x \sin(1/x) \to 0$, but $-\cos(1/x)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ in every neighborhood of $0$.
Therefore, $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x)$ does not exist.
Since $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x) \ne f'(0) = 0$, $f'$ is discontinuous at $0$.

**Conclusion:** Differentiability guarantees $f$ is continuous (<Ref id="diff-implies-cont" />), but does **not** guarantee $f'$ is continuous.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-chain-rule-proof-gap" type="prove" level="synthesis" title="The False Proof and Flaw in the Chain Rule" challenge={true} covers={['derivatives']}>

A student writes the following proof of <Ref id="chain-rule" />:
$$\frac{f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x))}{h} = \frac{f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x))}{g(x+h) - g(x)} \cdot \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h}.$$
Taking $h \to 0$, the first term approaches $f'(g(x))$ and the second approaches $g'(x)$, giving $f'(g(x))g'(x)$.
Diagnose the logical flaw in this argument. How does Carathéodory's definition or an auxiliary function resolve it?

<ExerciseSolution hint="What happens if $g(x+h) = g(x)$ for values of $h$ arbitrarily close to $0$? Can we divide by zero in the difference quotient?">

**The Flaw:**
The proof divides by $g(x+h) - g(x)$. However, $g$ may be constant in a neighborhood of $x$, or worse, oscillate infinitely often such that $g(x+h) = g(x)$ for points $h_n \to 0$ (for instance, $g(t) = x + t^2 \sin(1/t)$). For such $h$, the fraction $\frac{f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x))}{g(x+h) - g(x)}$ is undefined ($0/0$). One cannot divide by a quantity that may vanish along the limit sequence.

**The Resolution (Carathéodory's approach):**
Define the auxiliary difference-quotient slope function $\Phi$ on the domain of $f$:
$$\Phi(y) = \begin{cases} \frac{f(y) - f(u)}{y - u} & \text{if } y \ne u \\ f'(u) & \text{if } y = u \end{cases}$$
where $u = g(x)$.
Because $f$ is differentiable at $u$, $\lim_{y \to u} \Phi(y) = f'(u) = \Phi(u)$, so $\Phi$ is **continuous** at $u$.
Notice that $f(y) - f(u) = \Phi(y)(y - u)$ holds unconditionally for **all** $y$, even when $y = u$!
Now substitute $y = g(x+h)$:
$$\frac{f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x))}{h} = \Phi(g(x+h)) \cdot \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h}.$$
Since $g$ is differentiable at $x$, it is continuous at $x$, so $\lim_{h \to 0} g(x+h) = g(x) = u$.
Because $\Phi$ is continuous at $u$, $\lim_{h \to 0} \Phi(g(x+h)) = \Phi(u) = f'(g(x))$.
Multiplying limits:
$$(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x).$$
This proof never divides by $g(x+h) - g(x)$ and works unconditionally for all differentiable functions.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-central-difference-truncation" type="tool" level="application" title="Central Difference Truncation Error Analysis" covers={['derivatives', 'mean-value-theorem']}>

Suppose $f$ is three times differentiable on an open interval containing $x$ with $f'''$ continuous at $x$. Complete the numerical central difference formula below and prove using <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> that its truncation error satisfies
$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} - f'(x)}{h^2} = \frac{f'''(x)}{6},$$
establishing that the central difference error is $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(h)$.

<CodeExercise
  client:load
  title="Complete the Numerical Derivative (Central Difference)"
  code={`def central_difference(f, x, h=1e-5):
    # Central difference formula provides O(h^2) accuracy compared to forward O(h)
    return ([!blank!] - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)`}
  answers={["f(x + h)"]}
  explanation="The central difference formula is (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2h), which cancels the first-order error term and leaves O(h²) truncation error."
/>

<ExerciseSolution hint="Rewrite the ratio as $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h) - 2hf'(x)}{2h^3}$ and apply L'Hôpital's Rule with respect to $h$ three times.">

**Derivation of Truncation Error via L'Hôpital's Rule:**
Combine the numerator over the common denominator $2h^3$:
$$E(h) = \frac{\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} - f'(x)}{h^2} = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h) - 2hf'(x)}{2h^3}.$$
1. **First application of <Ref id="lhopital-rule" />:** As $h \to 0$, both numerator and denominator approach $0$. Differentiating with respect to $h$ (using <Ref id="chain-rule" /> on $f(x-h)$):
   $$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h) - 2hf'(x)}{2h^3} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(x+h) + f'(x-h) - 2f'(x)}{6h^2}.$$
2. **Second application of <Ref id="lhopital-rule" />:** At $h = 0$, the numerator is $f'(x) + f'(x) - 2f'(x) = 0$ and the denominator is $0$. Differentiating with respect to $h$ again:
   $$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f''(x+h) - f''(x-h)}{12h}.$$
3. **Evaluate via <Ref id="derivative-definition" /> (or a third L'Hôpital step):**
   $$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{f''(x+h) - f''(x-h)}{2h} = \frac{1}{6} f'''(x).$$
Thus $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} - f'(x) = \frac{f'''(x)}{6}h^2 + o(h^2) = \mathcal{O}(h^2)$, whereas the one-sided forward difference $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} - f'(x)$ has first-order error $\frac{f''(x)}{2}h + o(h) = \mathcal{O}(h)$.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-caratheodory-equivalence" type="formalise" level="transfer" title="Carathéodory Equivalence Formulation" covers={['derivatives']}>

Carathéodory's Theorem states: $f$ is differentiable at $c$ if and only if there exists a function $\phi$ continuous at $c$ such that $f(x) - f(c) = \phi(x)(x - c)$ for all $x$. Prove that if such a $\phi$ exists, then $f'(c) = \phi(c)$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Take the limit as $x \to c$ of $(f(x) - f(c))/(x - c)$ and use the continuity of $\phi$.">

**Proof:**
Suppose there exists a function $\phi$ continuous at $c$ such that:
$$f(x) - f(c) = \phi(x)(x - c) \quad \text{for all } x.$$
For any $x \ne c$, we can divide by $x - c$:
$$\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \phi(x).$$
Now evaluate the limit as $x \to c$ using <Ref id="derivative-definition" />:
$$f'(c) = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \lim_{x \to c} \phi(x).$$
Because $\phi$ is assumed continuous at $c$, <Ref id="continuity-definition" /> guarantees that $\lim_{x \to c} \phi(x) = \phi(c)$.
Therefore, the limit exists and:
$$f'(c) = \phi(c).$$
Conversely, if $f$ is differentiable at $c$, defining $\phi(x) = \frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}$ for $x \ne c$ and $\phi(c) = f'(c)$ yields a function continuous at $c$ that satisfies the equation for all $x$. ∎

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="prob-mvt-error-bound" type="construct" level="synthesis" title="Second-Order Remainder Bound via Cauchy Mean Value Theorem" covers={['derivatives', 'mean-value-theorem']}>

Let $f$ be twice differentiable on $[a, b]$, and let $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ be the linear approximation at $a$. Prove that for any $x \in (a, b]$, there exists $c \in (a, x)$ such that:
$$f(x) - L(x) = \frac{f''(c)}{2}(x - a)^2.$$

<ExerciseSolution hint="Apply Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem to $F(t) = f(x) - f(t) - f'(t)(x - t)$ and $G(t) = (x - t)^2$ on $[a, x]$.">

Define two auxiliary functions on $[a, x]$:
$$F(t) = f(x) - f(t) - f'(t)(x - t),$$
$$G(t) = (x - t)^2.$$
Evaluate at $t = x$:
$F(x) = f(x) - f(x) - f'(x)(0) = 0$, and $G(x) = (x - x)^2 = 0$.
Evaluate at $t = a$:
$F(a) = f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x - a) = f(x) - L(x)$, and $G(a) = (x - a)^2$.
Now differentiate with respect to $t$:
$$F'(t) = -f'(t) - [f''(t)(x - t) - f'(t)] = -f''(t)(x - t),$$
$$G'(t) = -2(x - t).$$
Note that for $t \in (a, x)$, $G'(t) = -2(x - t) \ne 0$.
By Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem (<Ref id="cauchy-mvt" />), there exists $c \in (a, x)$ such that:
$$\frac{F(x) - F(a)}{G(x) - G(a)} = \frac{F'(c)}{G'(c)}.$$
Substituting the values:
$$\frac{0 - (f(x) - L(x))}{0 - (x - a)^2} = \frac{-f''(c)(x - c)}{-2(x - c)} = \frac{f''(c)}{2}.$$
Multiplying by $(x - a)^2$:
$$f(x) - L(x) = \frac{f''(c)}{2}(x - a)^2.$$
This establishes Lagrange's form of the Taylor remainder for degree $1$.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-lhopital-circular" type="negate" level="transfer" title="Circular Reasoning in Differentiation of Sine" challenge={true} covers={['mean-value-theorem']}>

Explain why using <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> to evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1} = 1$ is circular reasoning when defining the derivative of sine.

<ExerciseSolution hint="How is the derivative of $\sin x$ proved in the first place? Inspect the difference quotient of sine.">

**The Circularity:**
To apply <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> to $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$, one must differentiate $\sin x$ at $x = 0$ to get $\cos 0$.
However, by <Ref id="derivative-definition" />, the derivative of $\sin x$ at $0$ is defined as:
$$\sin'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(0 + h) - \sin 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h}.$$
Thus, knowing that $\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x$ **already requires knowing that $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h} = 1$**!
Using L'Hôpital's Rule to prove that $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ assumes the very truth of the fact being evaluated.
The limit must be proved independently using geometry and the Squeeze Theorem (<Ref id="squeeze-theorem" />).

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-vector-mvt" type="describe" level="transfer" title="Failure of Equality in Vector-Valued MVT" covers={['mean-value-theorem']}>

Let $\mathbf{f}: [0, 2\pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be given by $\mathbf{f}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$.
Explain why there is no point $c \in (0, 2\pi)$ satisfying $\mathbf{f}'(c) = \frac{\mathbf{f}(2\pi) - \mathbf{f}(0)}{2\pi - 0}$. What inequality replaces the MVT in higher dimensions?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Compute the secant vector and the velocity vector $\|\mathbf{f}'(t)\|$.">

1. The secant vector is:
   $$\frac{\mathbf{f}(2\pi) - \mathbf{f}(0)}{2\pi} = \frac{(1, 0) - (1, 0)}{2\pi} = (0, 0).$$
2. The derivative vector is $\mathbf{f}'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$.
3. Its Euclidean norm is:
   $$\|\mathbf{f}'(t)\| = \sqrt{(-\sin t)^2 + (\cos t)^2} = \sqrt{1} = 1 \quad \text{for all } t \in [0, 2\pi].$$
4. Since the velocity vector is everywhere of length $1$, it is never equal to the zero vector $(0, 0)$. Thus no such $c$ exists.
5. In vector and multivariable analysis, equality is replaced by the **Mean Value Inequality**:
   $$\|\mathbf{f}(b) - \mathbf{f}(a)\| \le (b - a) \sup_{c \in (a, b)} \|\mathbf{f}'(c)\|.$$

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="ex-disconnected-domain" type="invented-definition" level="synthesis" title="The Open Interval Hypothesis and Disconnected Domains" challenge={true} covers={['mean-value-theorem']}>

In <Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" />, the hypothesis states that $f'(x) = 0$ on an **open interval $I$**.
Construct an example of a function $f$ whose derivative is identically zero everywhere on its domain, but $f$ is **not** constant. Why does this not contradict <Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" />?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Consider a domain that consists of two disjoint intervals, such as $(0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$.">

**Construction:**
Let the domain be $D = (0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$. Define $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ by:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 5 & \text{if } x \in (0, 1) \\ 9 & \text{if } x \in (2, 3) \end{cases}$$
1. On $(0, 1)$, $f$ is constant, so $f'(x) = 0$.
2. On $(2, 3)$, $f$ is constant, so $f'(x) = 0$.
Thus $f'(x) = 0$ for **all** $x \in D$.
However, $f$ is not constant on $D$: $f(0.5) = 5 \ne 9 = f(2.5)$.

**Why <Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" /> is not contradicted:**
<Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" /> requires the domain to be a **single connected interval** $I$.
If $x_1 \in (0, 1)$ and $x_2 \in (2, 3)$, the line segment $[x_1, x_2]$ is not contained in $D$ (it contains the gap $[1, 2]$ where $f$ is undefined).
Because the domain is disconnected, <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" /> cannot be applied across the gap. The proof of <Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" /> strictly requires $[x_1, x_2] \subseteq D$.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="prob-cauchy-lhopital-proof" type="prove" level="synthesis" title="Rigorous Proof of L'Hôpital's Rule for the 0/0 Form" covers={['limits', 'mean-value-theorem']}>

Assume $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $(c, b)$ with $g'(x) \ne 0$, and $\lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} g(x) = 0$.
Assume further that $\lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = L \in \mathbb{R}$.
Prove that $\lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Extend $f$ and $g$ continuously to $c$ by defining $f(c) = g(c) = 0$, then apply Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem on $[c, x]$.">

Extend $f$ and $g$ to $[c, b)$ by defining $f(c) = 0$ and $g(c) = 0$.
Since $\lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = 0 = f(c)$ and $\lim_{x \to c^+} g(x) = 0 = g(c)$, both functions are continuous on $[c, x]$ for every $x \in (c, b)$.
Both functions are differentiable on $(c, x)$, and $g'(t) \ne 0$ on $(c, x)$.
By Rolle's Theorem, $g(x) - g(c) = g(x) \ne 0$ (otherwise $g'$ would vanish).
By Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem (<Ref id="cauchy-mvt" />), there exists some $\xi_x \in (c, x)$ such that:
$$\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{g(x) - g(c)} = \frac{f'(\xi_x)}{g'(\xi_x)}.$$
Now consider the limit as $x \to c^+$.
Since $c < \xi_x < x$, the Squeeze Theorem (<Ref id="squeeze-theorem" />) forces $\lim_{x \to c^+} \xi_x = c^+$.
Therefore:
$$\lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f'(\xi_x)}{g'(\xi_x)} = \lim_{\xi \to c^+} \frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)} = L.$$
This completes the proof of <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> for the right-sided $0/0$ form. ∎

</ExerciseSolution>

</Problem>

<Problem id="prob-darboux-derivative-roots" type="prove" level="transfer" title="Root Interlacing Between Function and Derivative" covers={['derivatives', 'mean-value-theorem']}>

Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be twice differentiable.
1. Prove that between any two real roots of $f$, there exists at least one real root of $f'$.
2. Deduce that if $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, then $f$ can have at most two real roots.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Apply Rolle's Theorem to consecutive roots of $f$, then examine monotonicity of $f'$ from the sign of $f''$.">

**Part 1: Interlacing of roots.**
Let $r_1 < r_2$ be two distinct real roots of $f$, so $f(r_1) = f(r_2) = 0$.
Since $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, it is continuous on $[r_1, r_2]$ and differentiable on $(r_1, r_2)$.
By Rolle's Theorem (<Ref id="rolles-theorem" />), there exists at least one $c \in (r_1, r_2)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.

**Part 2: Bound on number of roots.**
Suppose for contradiction that $f$ has three or more distinct roots $r_1 < r_2 < r_3$.
By Part 1, there exists $c_1 \in (r_1, r_2)$ with $f'(c_1) = 0$, and there exists $c_2 \in (r_2, r_3)$ with $f'(c_2) = 0$.
Since $r_1 < c_1 < r_2 < c_2 < r_3$, we have $c_1 < c_2$.
Now apply Rolle's Theorem to $f'$ on $[c_1, c_2]$: since $f'$ is continuous on $[c_1, c_2]$ and differentiable on $(c_1, c_2)$ with $f'(c_1) = f'(c_2) = 0$, there exists $\xi \in (c_1, c_2)$ such that:
$$f''(\xi) = 0.$$
However, the hypothesis states that $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
This contradiction proves that $f$ cannot have three or more roots; it can have at most two real roots.

</ExerciseSolution>
