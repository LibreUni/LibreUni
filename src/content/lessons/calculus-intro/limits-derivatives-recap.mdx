This recap sheet synthesises the foundational results of differential calculus developed across Chapter 1. It consolidates formal definitions, core theorems, decision criteria for method selection, critical failure modes when hypotheses fail, and pervasive operational errors.

## Definitions and Results

### 1. Limits and Continuity

* **Formal Limit Definition** (<Ref id="limit-definition" />): For $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ and limit point $c$, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. The punctured neighbourhood guarantees that $f(c)$ need not exist nor equal $L$.
* **Algebra of Limits** (<Ref id="algebra-of-limits" />): If $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$, limits commute linearly with addition ($\lim(f+g) = L+M$), scalar multiplication ($\lim(kf) = kL$), product ($\lim(fg) = LM$), and quotient ($\lim(f/g) = L/M$, provided $M \neq 0$).
* **Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem** (<Ref id="squeeze-theorem" />): If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ on a punctured neighbourhood of $c$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$, then $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$. Used to bound oscillating functions such as $x^2 \sin(1/x)$ between envelopes $\pm x^2$.

<Figure id="fig-squeeze-xsin" size="thumb" />

* **Continuity** (<Ref id="continuity-definition" />): A function $f$ is continuous at $c \in D$ if $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$. Graphically, no jumps, holes, or essential oscillations occur at $c$.
* **Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)** (<Ref id="ivt" />): If $f \in C([a, b])$ and $u$ lies strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = u$. Guarantees roots when $f(a)f(b) < 0$.
* **Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)** (<Ref id="evt" />): If $f \in C([a, b])$ on a closed, bounded interval, then $f$ is bounded and attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a, b]$.

### 2. The Derivative and Differentiation Rules

* **Derivative Definition** (<Ref id="derivative-definition" />): The instantaneous rate of change is defined by $f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{z \to x} \frac{f(z) - f(x)}{z - x}$, whenever this limit exists. Geometrically, it is the slope of the unique tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $(x, f(x))$.
* **Differentiability Implies Continuity** (<Ref id="diff-implies-cont" />): If $f'(c)$ exists, then $f$ is continuous at $c$. The converse fails: continuity does not imply differentiability (e.g., sharp corners, cusps, or infinite oscillations).
* **Linearity and Product/Quotient Rules** (<Ref id="differentiation-rules" />): Differentiation is linear: $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$. The Leibniz product rule gives $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, and the quotient rule gives $(f/g)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ where $g(x) \neq 0$.
* **Chain Rule** (<Ref id="chain-rule" />): If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, the composition $h = f \circ g$ is differentiable at $x$ with $h'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$.

### 3. Mean Value Theorems and Asymptotic Analysis

* **Rolle's Theorem** (<Ref id="rolles-theorem" />): If $f \in C([a, b])$, $f$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$, there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.
* **Mean Value Theorem (MVT)** (<Ref id="mean-value-theorem" />): If $f \in C([a, b])$ and $f$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ satisfying $f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$. The instantaneous rate matches the average rate over the interval.
* **Cauchy's Extended Mean Value Theorem** (<Ref id="cauchy-mvt" />): If $f, g \in C([a, b])$ are differentiable on $(a, b)$ with $g'(x) \neq 0$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)}$.
* **Zero-Derivative Characterisation** (<Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" />): If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x$ in an open interval $I$, then $f$ is constant on $I$. Consequently, two functions with identical derivatives on $I$ differ by a constant.
* **L'Hôpital's Rule** (<Ref id="lhopital-rule" />): For indeterminate forms of type $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}$, if $\lim \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = L \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{\pm\infty\}$ and $g'(x) \neq 0$ near $c$, then $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L$.



## Method Chooser

| Problem Shape / Goal | Recommended Tool | Step-by-Step Execution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$ (algebraic polynomials)** | Factorisation / Conjugate Multiplication | Factor out $(x-c)$ or multiply by the conjugate radical $\sqrt{P(x)} + \sqrt{Q(x)}$ to cancel vanishing denominators before evaluating. |
| **Indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ (transcendental functions)** | <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> | Verify that both numerator and denominator simultaneously vanish or diverge. Differentiate numerator and denominator separately until the form becomes determinate. |
| **Oscillating bounded factor (e.g., $\sin(1/x)$ or $(-1)^n$)** | <Ref id="squeeze-theorem" /> | Establish rigorous upper and lower bounds $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ where $\lim g(x) = \lim h(x) = L$. |
| **Prove existence of at least one root $f(x) = 0$** | <Ref id="ivt" /> | Verify $f \in C([a, b])$. Find points $a, b$ with $f(a)f(b) < 0$. Conclude existence of $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$. |
| **Prove existence of a unique root** | <Ref id="ivt" /> + <Ref id="rolles-theorem" /> | Use IVT for existence. For uniqueness, assume two roots $x_1 < x_2$; by Rolle's theorem, $f'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (x_1, x_2)$. If $f'(x) > 0$ or $f'(x) < 0$ strictly, this yields a contradiction. |
| **Bound error / prove global inequalities $\lvert f(b) - f(a) \rvert \le M\lvert b-a \rvert$** | <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" /> | Express $f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b - a)$ for some $c \in (a, b)$. Apply uniform bounds $\sup_{x} \lvert f'(x) \rvert \le M$ to obtain Lipschitz bounds. |
| **Differentiate nested functions $f(g(h(x)))$** | <Ref id="chain-rule" /> | Decompose into composition layers. Work strictly from the outermost derivative to innermost: $f'(g(h(x))) \cdot g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x)$. |



## Hypothesis Checklist

Every hypothesis in analysis is sharp. Omitting even one condition causes the conclusion to fail.

* **Extreme Value Theorem (<Ref id="evt" />)**
  * *Missing Closed Interval:* Let $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ on $(0, 1]$. Continuous on $(0, 1]$, but unbounded as $x \to 0^+$; no maximum exists. For a bounded example, $f(x) = x$ on $(0, 1)$ has $\sup f = 1$ and $\inf f = 0$, but attains neither.
  * *Missing Bounded Interval:* Let $f(x) = x$ on $[0, \infty)$. Continuous and closed, but unbounded; no maximum exists.
  * *Missing Continuity:* Let $f(x) = x$ for $x \in [0, 1)$ and $f(1) = 0$. Defined on compact $[0, 1]$, but discontinuous at $x = 1$. The supremum is $1$, but $f(x) < 1$ for all $x \in [0, 1]$; no maximum is attained.

* **Intermediate Value Theorem (<Ref id="ivt" />)**
  * *Missing Continuity:* Let $f(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$ (with $f(0) = 1$). $f(-1) = -1 < 0$ and $f(1) = 1 > 0$, but $f(x)$ never equals $0$.

* **Zero-Derivative Characterisation (<Ref id="zero-derivative-constant" />)**
  * *Missing Connected Domain:* Let $D = (0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$. Define $f(x) = 0$ on $(0, 1)$ and $f(x) = 5$ on $(2, 3)$. Then $f'(x) = 0$ for every $x \in D$, yet $f$ is not constant on $D$. Differentiability implies constancy only on connected components (intervals).

* **Mean Value Theorem (<Ref id="mean-value-theorem" />)**
  * *Missing Continuity on $[a, b]$ (Endpoint discontinuity):* Let $f(x) = 0$ for $x \in [0, 1)$ and $f(1) = 1$. $f$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$ with $f'(x) = 0$. The secant slope is $\frac{1 - 0}{1 - 0} = 1$. No point in $(0, 1)$ has $f'(c) = 1$.
  * *Missing Differentiability on $(a, b)$:* Let $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ on $[-1, 1]$ (see the cusp counterexample below). Then $f \in C([-1, 1])$ and the secant slope is $\frac{f(1) - f(-1)}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0$. However, $f'(0)$ fails to exist at the vertical cusp $x = 0$, and $f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} \ne 0$ for all $x \ne 0$; no $c \in (-1, 1)$ achieves $f'(c) = 0$.

## Typical Errors

1. **Unchecked L'Hôpital Application:** Differentiating numerator and denominator when the quotient is determinate. For example, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x + 2}{x + 1} = 2$, but applying L'Hôpital yields $\frac{1}{0}$, which is completely erroneous.
2. **Confusing Continuity with Differentiability:** Assuming that because $f$ has no holes or jumps, its derivative must exist. The Weierstrass function is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere; $f(x) = |x|$ is non-differentiable at $x=0$.
3. **Assuming $f'$ is Continuous:** A function can be differentiable everywhere while its derivative is discontinuous. The classic counterexample $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = 0$ has $f'(0) = 0$, but $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x)$ does not exist due to high-frequency oscillation.
4. **Locating $c$ at the Boundary:** Asserting that $c = a$ or $c = b$ satisfies Rolle's theorem or MVT. The theorems strictly state $c \in (a, b)$ in the **open** interval.
5. **Treating $\frac{df}{dx}$ as an Algebraic Fraction:** While Leibniz notation is suggestive, manipulating differentials algebraically without justifying the chain rule or inverse function theorem leads to severe errors in multivariable or implicit settings.
