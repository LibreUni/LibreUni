How fast is a falling stone travelling *at a single instant*? Average velocity over a time interval is easy — divide distance by duration — but at one instant both quantities are zero, and $0/0$ is undefined. The same tension appears everywhere in calculus: tangent slopes, areas, infinite sums. Each is a **limit**: the value a quantity approaches as the input approaches a target.

This lesson develops the limit concept precisely enough to *prove* things about it, then builds the two structural theorems of continuous functions — the Intermediate Value Theorem and the Extreme Value Theorem — that the rest of calculus depends on.

**Prerequisites.** Inequalities, absolute value, and the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ (every nonempty bounded set has a supremum). No derivative or integral machinery is used.


## The Formal Definition of a Limit

Intuitively, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ means we can make $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ by making $x$ sufficiently close to $c$. The obstacle in making this precise is circularity: "sufficiently close" must not depend on the value $f(c)$, which may not even exist. The resolution, due to Weierstrass, is to quantify the two tolerances and order them: **for every** output tolerance $\epsilon$, **there exists** an input tolerance $\delta$ — and $\delta$ must be chosen *before* seeing any particular $x$.

<MathStatement id="limit-definition" kind="definition" title="Limit of a Function" number="1">

Let $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ with $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, and let $c$ be a limit point of $D$ (every neighborhood of $c$ contains points of $D$ other than $c$). Then

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$$

if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in D$:

$$0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon.$$

</MathStatement>

Three boundary cases deserve explicit attention:

* The condition $0 < |x - c|$ **excludes $x = c$**. The limit ignores what happens *at* the point — $f$ need not even be defined there. This is exactly what lets tangent slopes and instantaneous velocity make sense.
* "$c$ is a limit point" prevents vacuous truth: if $c$ were isolated in $D$, some $\delta$-neighborhood would contain no other $x$, and the implication would hold trivially for *any* $L$.
* $\delta$ may depend on $\epsilon$ (and on $c$), but a single $\delta$ must work for **all** $x$ in the window simultaneously — the condition is uniform in $x$.

The definition is best internalized as a two-player game. Your opponent picks $\epsilon$, shrinking the target zone $L \pm \epsilon$ on the $y$-axis; you must respond with $\delta$, a window $c \pm \delta$ on the $x$-axis, so that *every* input from the window (except $c$) lands inside the target zone. You win if you have a response to *every* $\epsilon > 0$. [Spivak (4th ed.), Ch. 5 "Limits", §"Delta–Epsilon Proofs"]

### Worked Example 1: a linear function

**Claim.** $\lim_{x \to 2} (3x - 1) = 5$.

Given $\epsilon > 0$, we must force $|f(x) - 5| < \epsilon$ by bounding $|x - 2|$. Start from the quantity we control:

$$|f(x) - 5| = |(3x - 1) - 5| = |3x - 6| = 3\,|x - 2|.$$

So $|f(x) - 5| < \epsilon$ whenever $|x - 2| < \epsilon/3$. **Choose $\delta = \epsilon/3$.** Then $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$ gives $|f(x) - 5| = 3|x - 2| < 3\delta = \epsilon$, for every $x$ at once. The limit is proved.

Concretely: if the opponent demands $\epsilon = 0.3$, respond with $\delta = 0.1$; any $x$ within $0.1$ of $2$ gives $f(x)$ within $0.3$ of $5$.

### Worked Example 2: a nonlinear function

**Claim.** $\lim_{x \to 3} x^2 = 9$.

Here $|f(x) - L| = |x^2 - 9| = |x - 3|\,|x + 3|$: a *variable* factor multiplies the quantity we control. The trick is to freeze it. Impose the preliminary restriction $\delta \le 1$: then $|x - 3| < 1$ means $2 < x < 4$, so $|x + 3| < 7$. Now

$$|x - 3| < \delta \implies |x^2 - 9| = |x-3|\,|x+3| < 7\,\delta.$$

**Choose $\delta = \min(1, \epsilon/7)$.** Then $\delta \le 1$ activates the bound $|x+3| < 7$, and $7\delta \le \epsilon$ finishes: $|x^2 - 9| < \epsilon$ for every $x$ with $0 < |x-3| < \delta$.

Compare the two proofs. In Example 1 the response was proportional ($\delta = \epsilon/3$); in Example 2 it is not, because the sensitivity of $x^2$ to changes in $x$ varies *along* the window. The further $c$ is from $0$, the smaller the working $\delta$ for the same $\epsilon$ — the definition captures local sensitivity, not a global property of the formula.

## Fundamental Limit Theorems

Computing limits directly from the $\epsilon$-$\delta$ definition for every new function would be unusable. The algebra of limits lets us assemble complicated limits from simple ones, *provided* the pieces exist and the operations are defined.

<MathStatement id="algebra-of-limits" kind="theorem" title="Algebra of Limits" number="1" proofId="sum-limit-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: 'limit-definition', label: 'Definition 1' }]}>

Suppose $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$, and $c$ is a limit point of the relevant domains. Then:

1. $\lim_{x \to c} (f + g)(x) = L + M$;
2. $\lim_{x \to c} (f \cdot g)(x) = L \cdot M$;
3. if additionally $M \ne 0$, then $\lim_{x \to c} (f/g)(x) = L/M$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="sum-limit-proof" for="algebra-of-limits">

We prove the sum rule; the product and quotient rules follow the same pattern with an extra preliminary bound (see Exercise 6).

Let $\epsilon > 0$.

1. Since $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$, there exists $\delta_1 > 0$ such that $0 < |x - c| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon/2$.
2. Since $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$, there exists $\delta_2 > 0$ such that $0 < |x - c| < \delta_2 \implies |g(x) - M| < \epsilon/2$.
3. Set $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. For $0 < |x - c| < \delta$ both bounds hold, so the triangle inequality gives:

$$|(f+g)(x) - (L+M)| \le |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M| < \epsilon/2 + \epsilon/2 = \epsilon.$$

Thus $\lim_{x \to c} (f+g)(x) = L + M$.

</MathProof>

The **quotient rule's hypothesis $M \ne 0$ is essential and is the most common source of wrong limits**. The classic $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$ cannot be evaluated by the quotient rule: $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin x = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} x = 0$, so rule 3 does not apply. The correct value is $1$, but it must be proved by squeezing, not by algebra [Spivak (4th ed.), Ch. 15, §"The Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions"]. The algebra of limits never resolves a $0/0$ form; that is precisely the case calculus exists to handle.

<MathStatement id="squeeze-theorem" kind="theorem" title="Squeeze Theorem" number="2" proofId="squeeze-theorem-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: 'limit-definition', label: 'Definition 1' }]}>

Let $I$ be an open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $c$ itself. Let $f, g, h$ be functions defined on $I$. If for all $x \in I \setminus \{c\}$,

$$g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$$

and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$, then:

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L.$$

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="squeeze-theorem-proof" for="squeeze-theorem">

Let $\epsilon > 0$ be given.

1. Since $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = L$, there exists $\delta_1 > 0$ such that $0 < |x - c| < \delta_1 \implies |g(x) - L| < \epsilon \iff L - \epsilon < g(x) < L + \epsilon$.
2. Since $\lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$, there exists $\delta_2 > 0$ such that $0 < |x - c| < \delta_2 \implies |h(x) - L| < \epsilon \iff L - \epsilon < h(x) < L + \epsilon$.
3. Let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. For $0 < |x - c| < \delta$, both inequalities hold. Since $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$, we have:
$$L - \epsilon < g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x) < L + \epsilon.$$
Thus, $L - \epsilon < f(x) < L + \epsilon \iff |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$, proving $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$.

</MathProof>

The Squeeze Theorem is the standard tool for functions that *oscillate too fast to control directly*. A classic application: $\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin(1/x) = 0$. Since $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$ for all $x \ne 0$, multiplying by $x^2 > 0$ yields $-x^2 \le x^2 \sin(1/x) \le x^2$. Both bounds tend to $0$ as $x \to 0$, so the middle function does too — *regardless of how fast it oscillates inside the envelope*. The theorem transfers existence, not rates: it says nothing about *how fast* $f$ approaches $L$.

Contrast with a function the squeeze *cannot* rescue: $\sin(1/x)$ itself has no limit at $0$ — no envelope tighter than $[-1, 1]$ is available, and the values cycle through all of $[-1, 1]$ in every neighborhood of $0$ (see the counterexample below).


## Continuity

Continuity formalises "no gaps or jumps" — the input–output contract that makes the two structural theorems below possible.

<MathStatement id="continuity-definition" kind="definition" title="Continuity" number="2">

A function $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at a point $c \in D$ if:

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c).$$

The function is continuous on an interval $I \subseteq D$ if it is continuous at every $c \in I$.

</MathStatement>

Unpacking the definition: continuity at $c$ requires (i) $f(c)$ is defined, (ii) $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ exists, and (iii) the two are equal. Each requirement fails in a different, characteristic way, giving a taxonomy of discontinuities:

| Type | Definition failure | Canonical example at $0$ | Continuous extension possible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Removable** | (i) or (iii): limit exists but $f(c)$ is undefined or differs | $f(x) = \sin(x)/x$: limit $= 1$, $f(0)$ undefined | Yes: (re)define $f(0) = 1$ |
| **Jump** | (ii): one-sided limits exist but differ | $f(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$: limits $\mp 1$ | No |
| **Essential** | (ii): limit does not exist, even one-sided | $f(x) = \sin(1/x)$ | No |

Sums and products of continuous functions are continuous (transfer the Algebra of Limits termwise); **quotients are continuous only where the denominator is nonzero** — the quotient-rule hypothesis again.

### Counterexample: essential discontinuity

Consider $f(x) = \sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$. The limit as $x \to 0$ does not exist, so no definition of $f(0)$ can make $f$ continuous — the discontinuity is essential.

The boundary between "tameable" and "untameable" oscillation is exactly what a squeeze envelope can control — a distinction that returns when derivatives are defined.


## The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

Continuous functions on closed intervals cannot skip values. This is the theorem that guarantees roots exist before we compute them.

<MathStatement id="ivt" kind="theorem" title="Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)" number="3" dependsOn={[{ id: 'continuity-definition', label: 'Definition 2' }]}>

If $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $u$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$f(c) = u.$$

</MathStatement>

The proof [Spivak (4th ed.), Ch. 8 "Least Upper Bound", Theorem 1; Rudin, *PMA* (3rd ed.), Ch. 4, Thm 4.23] constructs $c = \sup\{x \in [a,b] : f(x) < u\}$ — a set that is nonempty and bounded by completeness — and shows continuity forces $f(c) = u$ exactly. Completeness is not decoration: on $\mathbb{Q}$ the theorem fails ($f(x) = x^2$ is continuous on $[1,2] \cap \mathbb{Q}$, takes endpoint values $1$ and $4$, but no rational has square $2$).

**Both hypotheses are load-bearing:**

* *Discontinuity:* $f(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$ jumps from $-1$ to $1$ and never takes the value $u = 0$ between its endpoint values.
* *Endpoint values:* the theorem cannot even be *stated* without $f(a)$, $f(b)$ — this is where "closed interval" enters.

The IVT also gives a *numerical* algorithm. If $f$ is continuous and $f(a)$, $f(b)$ have opposite signs, the sign change survives on exactly one half of $[a,b]$ after bisecting at $m = (a+b)/2$, and that half must contain a root. Repeating halves the error each step: after $n$ bisections the root is pinned to within $(b-a)/2^n$, so a tolerance $\tau$ is guaranteed by any $n \ge \log_2((b-a)/\tau)$. [Burden & Faires, *Numerical Analysis* (9th ed.), §2.1 "The Bisection Method"]

<CodeRunner
  client:load
  code={`def bisection_method(f, a, b, tol=1e-5):
    if f(a) * f(b) >= 0:
        raise ValueError("IVT condition not met: f(a) and f(b) must have opposite signs.")

    while (b - a) / 2.0 > tol:
        midpoint = (a + b) / 2.0
        if f(midpoint) == 0:
            return midpoint
        elif f(a) * f(midpoint) < 0:
            b = midpoint
        else:
            a = midpoint
    return (a + b) / 2.0

# Find the root of f(x) = x^3 - x - 2 on [1, 2].
# Check: f(1) = -2 < 0 < f(2) = 4, so the IVT guarantees a root in (1, 2).
f = lambda x: x**3 - x - 2
root = bisection_method(f, 1, 2)
print(f"Calculated Root: {root:.6f}")
print(f"f(root) = {f(root):.6f}")
`}
/>

**Verify the error bound.** The loop exits when $(b-a)/2 \le 10^{-5}$; since the interval starts with length $1$ and each pass halves it, after $n$ passes the length is $2^{-n}$, and $2^{-17} < 10^{-5}$. So the printed root is within $10^{-5}$ of a true root of $x^3 - x - 2$ — a claim about the *real* root, justified by the IVT plus halving arithmetic, not by inspecting the output.


## The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)

Continuity on a closed, bounded interval does more than prevent skipped values — it keeps $f$ bounded and forces the bounds to be *attained*. [Spivak (4th ed.), Ch. 7 "Three Hard Theorems", Thms 1–2; Rudin, *PMA* (3rd ed.), Ch. 4, Thms 4.16–4.17]

<MathStatement id="evt" kind="theorem" title="Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)" number="4" dependsOn={[{ id: 'continuity-definition', label: 'Definition 2' }]}>

If $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, then $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum: there exist points $x_{\min}, x_{\max} \in [a, b]$ such that

$$f(x_{\min}) \le f(x) \le f(x_{\max}) \quad \text{for all } x \in [a, b].$$

</MathStatement>

The theorem has three hypotheses — continuity, closedness, boundedness of the interval — and each is falsifiable by a concrete counterexample:

* **Open interval fails.** $f(x) = x$ on $(0, 1)$ is continuous and bounded ($0 < f < 1$), but no point attains $0$ or $1$: the supremum and infimum live only "at the missing endpoints."
* **Unbounded interval fails.** $f(x) = x$ on $[0, \infty)$ is continuous but unbounded above.
* **Discontinuity fails.** Define $f(x) = x$ on the open part $(0, 1)$ and $f(0) = f(1) = -1$. This $f$ is bounded on a closed interval, but its values approach $1$ from below without ever attaining it: the jump at the endpoints dodges the supremum, so no maximum exists.

Like the IVT, the EVT is a completeness theorem: its proof [Spivak (4th ed.), Ch. 8; Rudin, *PMA*, Ch. 4, Thm 4.16] shows the set $f([a,b])$ is bounded and that $\sup f([a,b])$ is a value of $f$ — both steps requiring least upper bounds.

The IVT and EVT are complementary guarantees: the IVT says the image $f([a,b])$ has *no gaps between attained values*, the EVT says the image is *bounded and contains its own endpoints*. Together they make the image of a compact interval a closed, bounded — and, for the derivative-theorems to come, decisive — object.

### Exercise 1 (Application)

Prove directly from Definition 1 that $\lim_{x \to 1} (2x + 3) = 5$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Compute $|f(x) - 5|$ in terms of $|x - 1|$ first; the constant $3$ is irrelevant to the choice of $\delta$.">

Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta = \epsilon/2$. Then $0 < |x - 1| < \delta$ gives

$$|(2x + 3) - 5| = |2x - 2| = 2\,|x - 1| < 2\delta = \epsilon.$$

**Rubric.** Full credit requires: (a) an explicit $\delta$ depending on $\epsilon$; (b) the implication stated for *all* $x$ with $0 < |x-1| < \delta$, not just one; (c) the chain of equalities ending in a quantity $< \epsilon$. A response that verifies the limit only numerically (e.g., plugging in $x = 1.01$) earns no credit.

</ExerciseSolution>

### Exercise 2 (Application)

Prove from Definition 1 that $\lim_{x \to 2} x^2 = 4$, giving $\delta$ explicitly in terms of $\epsilon$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Factor $|x^2 - 4| = |x-2|\,|x+2|$ and freeze the variable factor with a preliminary bound $\delta \le 1$, as in Worked Example 2.">

Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta = \min(1, \epsilon/5)$. If $|x - 2| < \delta \le 1$, then $1 < x < 3$, so $|x + 2| < 5$, and

$$|x^2 - 4| = |x-2|\,|x+2| < 5\,\delta \le 5 \cdot \frac{\epsilon}{5} = \epsilon.$$

**Rubric.** Any correct $\delta$ (e.g., $\min(1, \epsilon/5)$) earns full credit; a response of $\delta = \epsilon/5$ *without* the preliminary bound is a **defect**, because $|x+2| < 5$ was used without justification — that inequality needs $|x-2| < 1$. A response of $\delta = \epsilon/4$ is falsifiable: with $\epsilon = 0.4$, take $x = 2.1$, then $|x^2 - 4| = 0.41 > 0.4$.

</ExerciseSolution>

### Exercise 3 (Transfer)

Define $f(x) = x \sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$. Show that $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 0$, and state where $f$ can be extended continuously. Then contrast: does $\sin(1/x)$ have a limit at $0$?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Bound $|x \sin(1/x)|$ by a quantity that ignores the sine, then apply the Squeeze Theorem (Theorem 2).">

For $x \ne 0$: $|f(x)| = |x|\,|\sin(1/x)| \le |x| \cdot 1 = |x|$, since $|\sin t| \le 1$ for all $t$. Both bound functions $\mp|x|$ tend to $0$ as $x \to 0$, so the Squeeze Theorem gives $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 0$. Since $f(0)$ is undefined but the limit exists, the extension $F(0) := 0$ makes $F$ continuous at $0$ (removable discontinuity). By contrast $\sin(1/x)$ has no limit at $0$: its values cycle through $[-1, 1]$ in every neighborhood, so no candidate $L$ survives $\epsilon = 1/2$.

**Rubric.** Requires: (a) the explicit bound $|\sin t| \le 1$ invoked with the squeeze; (b) the conclusion stated for the *extended* function; (c) a falsifying $\epsilon$ (any $\epsilon < 1$ works) for $\sin(1/x)$. Claiming "$\sin(1/x)$ is undefined at $0$, so no limit" is a **failure**: undefinedness at $c$ does not preclude a limit.

</ExerciseSolution>

### Exercise 4 (Transfer)

A continuous function $g$ on $[-1, 1]$ satisfies $g(-1) = -0.5$ and $g(1) = 0.5$. For each value $u$, decide whether the IVT guarantees a solution of $g(x) = u$ on $[-1, 1]$: **(a)** $u = 0$; **(b)** $u = 0.9$; **(c)** $u = -0.5$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Check each $u$ against the hypothesis: is $u$ between $g(-1)$ and $g(1)$?">

* **(a)** Yes: $-0.5 < 0 < 0.5$, so IVT guarantees some $c \in (-1, 1)$ with $g(c) = 0$.
* **(b)** No: $0.9$ is not between $g(-1) = -0.5$ and $g(1) = 0.5$. The IVT is silent — a solution may or may not exist depending on $g$ (if $g(x) = x$, none exists).
* **(c)** Yes — but for a different reason: $u = -0.5 = g(-1)$ is *attained at the endpoint*, not guaranteed inside $(a,b)$. The IVT as stated locates interior points for values strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$; endpoint values are already in the image by definition.

**Rubric.** Each part scores independently. For (a): correct yes/no with the hypothesis quoted. For (b): a bare "no" without the reason ("$u$ not between the endpoint values") loses credit; a "no, because $g$ is discontinuous" is a **failure** ($g$ is continuous by hypothesis). For (c): distinguishing *guaranteed by IVT* from *true but not via IVT* is the transfer step.

</ExerciseSolution>

### Exercise 5 (Synthesis)

Prove, using the IVT, that the equation $x = \cos x$ has a solution in $[0, 1]$. All inputs: define the auxiliary function yourself; $\cos$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ with $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\cos 1 \approx 0.54$ (you may use $\cos 1 < 1$).

<ExerciseSolution hint="Turn the fixed-point equation into a root-finding problem: move everything to one side and apply the IVT to the resulting continuous function.">

Let $h(x) = x - \cos x$. As a difference of continuous functions ($x$ and $\cos x$), $h$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$. At the endpoints: $h(0) = 0 - 1 = -1 < 0$, and $h(1) = 1 - \cos 1 > 0$ since $\cos 1 < 1$. So $u = 0$ lies strictly between $h(0)$ and $h(1)$, and the IVT guarantees some $c \in (0, 1)$ with $h(c) = 0$, i.e., $c = \cos c$.

**Rubric.** Requires: (a) an explicitly defined auxiliary function; (b) a stated continuity argument (sum/difference of continuous functions); (c) *strict* endpoint signs with justification ($\cos 1 < 1$, not "$\approx$"); (d) the IVT invoked on $u = 0$ between the endpoint values. Asserting "since $\cos$ is continuous it has a fixed point" without the sign change is a **failure** — continuity alone on a non-closed or badly-oriented domain does not give fixed points (compare $x \mapsto x + 1$).

</ExerciseSolution>

### Exercise 6 (Synthesis)

Complete the Algebra of Limits: assuming $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M \ne 0$, prove there is a $\delta_0 > 0$ such that $|g(x)| > |M|/2$ whenever $0 < |x - c| < \delta_0$; then use this to derive the quotient rule (Theorem 1.3) from the product and reciprocal rules.

<ExerciseSolution hint="First pin $g$ away from $0$ with $\epsilon = |M|/2$ in the limit definition; the same trick as Worked Example 2 — a preliminary bound — then controls the quotient $|1/g(x) - 1/M|$.">

**Step 1 (pin away from zero).** Apply the limit definition with $\epsilon = |M|/2$: there is $\delta_0 > 0$ such that $0 < |x-c| < \delta_0$ implies $|g(x) - M| < |M|/2$. Then by the reverse triangle inequality,

$$|g(x)| = |M + (g(x) - M)| \ge |M| - |g(x) - M| > |M| - \tfrac{|M|}{2} = \tfrac{|M|}{2} > 0.$$

**Step 2 (reciprocal).** For $0 < |x - c| < \delta_0$:

$$\left|\frac{1}{g(x)} - \frac{1}{M}\right| = \frac{|M - g(x)|}{|g(x)|\,|M|} < \frac{|g(x) - M|}{(|M|/2)\,|M|} = \frac{2}{|M|^2}\,|g(x) - M|.$$

Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta_1$ so that $|g(x) - M| < \epsilon\,|M|^2/2$ for $0 < |x - c| < \delta_1$, and set $\delta = \min(\delta_0, \delta_1)$. Then $|1/g(x) - 1/M| < \epsilon$ throughout, so $\lim_{x \to c} 1/g(x) = 1/M$.

**Step 3 (quotient).** The product rule (Theorem 1.2) applied to $f$ and $1/g$ gives $\lim f/g = L/M$. ∎

**Rubric.** Full credit requires all three steps: the preliminary pin $|g(x)| > |M|/2$ (with $\epsilon = |M|/2$ explicit), the reciprocal estimate with the $2/|M|^2$ constant appearing, and the final composition with the product rule. Omitting Step 1 and dividing by $|g(x)|$ "as if" it were a nonzero constant is the classic **failure** — the estimate is unjustified without the pin.

</ExerciseSolution>

## Scope Boundaries & Deliberate Omissions

* **One-sided limits and limits at infinity** are used informally (jump discontinuities, asymptotic behavior) but not formally defined; they are the same $\epsilon$-$\delta$ pattern with a restricted or unbounded domain and are developed with the derivative applications that need them.
* **Uniform continuity** is deliberately deferred: it requires comparing $\delta$s across *all* points at once, which is a new conceptual layer (compactness) beyond this lesson.
* **Sequential definitions of limits** (via $x_n \to c \Rightarrow f(x_n) \to L$; [Rudin, *PMA* (3rd ed.), Ch. 4, Thm 4.12]) are equivalent to Definition 1 but omitted here to keep a single formal thread; they return with series.
* **Formal proofs of the IVT and EVT** (via least upper bounds) are referenced with anchors above but not reproduced; they belong to the completeness treatment.
* **Nonstandard analysis** (infinitesimals) is not treated; the Weierstrass $\epsilon$-$\delta$ formalism is the working definition throughout the course.
