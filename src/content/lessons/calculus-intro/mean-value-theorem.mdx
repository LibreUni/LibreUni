The Mean Value Theorem (MVT) is one of the most critical theoretical tools in calculus. It establishes a direct connection between the average rate of change over an interval (global behavior) and the instantaneous rate of change at a point within that interval (local behavior).


## Rolle's Theorem: The Foundation

Before proving the general Mean Value Theorem, we must prove its special case, Rolle's Theorem.

<MathStatement id="rolles-theorem" kind="theorem" title="Rolle's Theorem" proofId="rolles-theorem-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "evt" }]}>

Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function that satisfies:
1. $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$.
2. $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$.
3. $f(a) = f(b)$.

Then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that:
$$f'(c) = 0.$$

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="rolles-theorem-proof" for="rolles-theorem">

By the Extreme Value Theorem (<Ref id="evt" />), the continuous function $f$ on the compact interval $[a, b]$ attains a global maximum $M$ and a global minimum $m$:
$$
\exists\, x_{\min}, x_{\max} \in [a, b] \quad \text{such that} \quad m = f(x_{\min}) \le f(x) \le f(x_{\max}) = M \quad \forall x \in [a, b].
$$

**Case 1: Constant function ($M = m$).**

If $M = m$, then $f$ is constant throughout $[a, b]$. Therefore:
$$
f'(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in (a, b),
$$
and *every* interior point $c \in (a, b)$ satisfies the theorem.

**Case 2: Non-trivial extremum in the interior ($M \neq m$).**

Because $f(a) = f(b)$, at least one of the extrema must occur at an interior point $c \in (a, b)$. Without loss of generality, suppose the maximum occurs in the interior: $f(c) = M$ for some $c \in (a, b)$.

Since $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, the numerator $f(x) - f(c) \le 0$ everywhere. We evaluate the one-sided difference quotients:

1. **Right-hand derivative ($x > c$):** Here $x - c > 0$, so dividing non-positive by positive yields a non-positive ratio:
$$
\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \le 0 \implies f'_+(c) = \lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \le 0.
$$

2. **Left-hand derivative ($x < c$):** Here $x - c < 0$, so dividing non-positive by negative reverses the inequality:
$$
\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \ge 0 \implies f'_-(c) = \lim_{x \to c^-} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \ge 0.
$$

Since $f$ is differentiable at $c$, both one-sided limits must agree with the two-sided derivative:
$$
f'(c) = f'_+(c) = f'_-(c).
$$
The only real value that satisfies both $f'(c) \le 0$ and $f'(c) \ge 0$ is:
$$
f'(c) = 0.
$$

</MathProof>

## The Mean Value Theorem

We generalize Rolle's Theorem to functions where $f(a) \neq f(b)$ by tilting the coordinate system.

<MathStatement id="mean-value-theorem" kind="theorem" title="Mean Value Theorem (MVT)" proofId="mvt-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "rolles-theorem" }]}>

Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function satisfying:
1. $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.
2. $f$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$.

Then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

</MathStatement>

<Figure id="fig-mvt-cubic" />

Does there necessarily exist $c \in (a, b)$ such that $\mathbf{f}'(c) = \frac{\mathbf{f}(b) - \mathbf{f}(a)}{b - a}$? Think of a particle moving along a circular path before opening the analysis below.

<ExerciseSolution

  title="Reveal analysis"

>

**Analysis:**
**No!** The Mean Value Theorem fails for vector-valued functions $\mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ ($n \ge 2$).
Consider $\mathbf{f}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$ on $[0, 2\pi]$.
The average velocity over one full orbit is:
$$\frac{\mathbf{f}(2\pi) - \mathbf{f}(0)}{2\pi - 0} = \frac{(1, 0) - (1, 0)}{2\pi} = (0, 0).$$
However, the velocity vector is $\mathbf{f}'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$, whose magnitude is $\|\mathbf{f}'(t)\| = \sqrt{(-\sin t)^2 + (\cos t)^2} = 1$ for all $t$. The instantaneous velocity never vanishes!
In higher dimensions, one can only obtain the *Mean Value Inequality*: $\|\mathbf{f}(b) - \mathbf{f}(a)\| \le (b - a) \sup_{t \in (a, b)} \|\mathbf{f}'(t)\|$.

</ExerciseSolution>

<MathProof id="mvt-proof" for="mean-value-theorem">

We construct an auxiliary function $g(x)$ measuring the vertical difference between the curve $y = f(x)$ and the secant chord passing through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$:

$$
g(x) = f(x) - \left[ f(a) + \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a) \right].
$$

We check the three hypotheses of Rolle's Theorem (<Ref id="rolles-theorem" />) on $[a, b]$:

1. **Continuity:** $g$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ because $f$ is continuous and the linear chord is continuous.
2. **Differentiability:** $g$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ because $f$ is differentiable and linear terms are smooth.
3. **Boundary conditions:** Evaluating $g$ at both endpoints:
$$
g(a) = f(a) - \left[ f(a) + 0 \right] = 0,
$$
$$
g(b) = f(b) - \left[ f(a) + \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(b - a) \right] = f(b) - f(b) = 0.
$$

Since $g(a) = g(b) = 0$, Rolle's Theorem guarantees that there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that:
$$
g'(c) = 0.
$$

Differentiating $g(x)$ directly:
$$
g'(x) = f'(x) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

Evaluating at $x = c$:
$$
0 = f'(c) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \implies f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

</MathProof>

## Critical Corollaries of the MVT

The MVT allows us to transition from local derivative properties to global function properties.

<MathStatement id="zero-derivative-constant" kind="corollary" title="Zero Derivative Implies Constant" proofId="zero-derivative-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "mean-value-theorem" }]}>

If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x$ in an open interval $I$, then $f$ is constant on $I$.

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="zero-derivative-proof" for="zero-derivative-constant">

Pick any two points $x_1, x_2 \in I$ with $x_1 < x_2$. Since $f$ is differentiable on $I$, it is continuous on $[x_1, x_2]$ and differentiable on $(x_1, x_2)$. By the MVT, there exists $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}.$$

Since $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in I$, we must have $f'(c) = 0$. Therefore,

$$\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = 0 \implies f(x_2) - f(x_1) = 0 \implies f(x_2) = f(x_1).$$

Since this holds for any $x_1, x_2 \in I$, $f$ is constant on $I$.

</MathProof>

> **Remark.** Even when a derivative $f'$ is discontinuous (such as the derivative of $x^2 \sin(1/x)$ at $0$), Darboux's theorem guarantees that $f'$ cannot have simple jump discontinuities: if $f$ is differentiable on $[a, b]$, then $f'$ attains every value between $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$.


## Cauchy's Generalized Mean Value Theorem

We can generalize the MVT to compare two functions parametrically.

<MathStatement id="cauchy-mvt" kind="theorem" title="Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem" proofId="cauchy-mvt-proof" dependsOn={[{ id: "rolles-theorem" }]}>

Let $f, g: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. If $g'(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$, then there exists some $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)}.$$

</MathStatement>

<MathProof id="cauchy-mvt-proof" for="cauchy-mvt">

First, note $g(b) - g(a) \neq 0$, because if $g(b) = g(a)$, Rolle's Theorem would imply there is a point where $g'(c) = 0$, violating the hypothesis. We define:

$$h(x) = [f(b) - f(a)]g(x) - [g(b) - g(a)]f(x).$$

1. $h$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.
2. Compute $h(a) = f(b)g(a) - g(b)f(a)$ and $h(b) = f(b)g(a) - g(b)f(a)$. Thus $h(a) = h(b)$.
3. By Rolle's Theorem, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $h'(c) = 0$:

$$h'(c) = [f(b) - f(a)]g'(c) - [g(b) - g(a)]f'(c) = 0.$$

Dividing by $g'(c)[g(b) - g(a)]$ yields:

$$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)}.$$

</MathProof>

### L'Hôpital's Rule

Cauchy's MVT is the fundamental tool used to prove L'Hôpital's Rule for indeterminate limits of the form $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$.

<MathStatement id="lhopital-rule" kind="theorem" title="L'Hôpital's Rule" dependsOn={[{ id: "cauchy-mvt" }]}>

Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable on an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$). If $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = 0$, and $g'(x) \neq 0$ for $x \neq c$, then:

$$\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},$$

provided the limit on the right exists.

</MathStatement>


### Worked Trace: Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem Calculation

To see Cauchy's MVT in practice, consider $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x^3$ on the interval $[1, 2]$.

1. **Verify Hypotheses**: Both $f$ and $g$ are polynomials, so they are continuous on $[1, 2]$ and differentiable on $(1, 2)$. Moreover, $g'(x) = 3x^2 > 0$ for all $x \in (1, 2)$, satisfying $g'(x) \neq 0$.

2. **Compute Secant Quotient**:
   $$\frac{f(2) - f(1)}{g(2) - g(1)} = \frac{2^2 - 1^2}{2^3 - 1^3} = \frac{4 - 1}{8 - 1} = \frac{3}{7}.$$

3. **Compute Derivative Ratio**:
   $$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{2c}{3c^2} = \frac{2}{3c}.$$

4. **Solve for $c$**:
   $$\frac{2}{3c} = \frac{3}{7} \implies 9c = 14 \implies c = \frac{14}{9} \approx 1.5556.$$

5. **Interior Verification**:
   Since $1 < \frac{14}{9} < 2$, the solution lies strictly in the open interior $(1, 2)$, exactly as guaranteed by the theorem.


### Counterexample & Failure Mode: Boundary Breakdown Under Weakened Hypotheses

The Mean Value Theorem requires both continuity on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiability on the open interval $(a, b)$. Violating either condition causes the theorem to collapse immediately.

1. **Failure of Differentiability (Interior Cusp)**:
   Consider $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-1, 1]$.
   * Continuity holds everywhere on $[-1, 1]$.
   * At $x = 0$, the left-derivative is $-1$ and right-derivative is $+1$, so $f$ is not differentiable at $x = 0$.
   * Secant slope: $\frac{f(1) - f(-1)}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0$.
   * Instantaneous derivative: $f'(x) = -1$ for $x < 0$, and $f'(x) = +1$ for $x > 0$.
   * **Result**: There is no point $c \in (-1, 1)$ where $f'(c) = 0$. The conclusion fails completely.

2. **Failure of Continuity (Endpoint Jump)**:
   Consider $f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x = 0 \\ 1 & x \in (0, 1] \end{cases}$ on $[0, 1]$.
   * $f$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$ with $f'(x) = 0$ everywhere.
   * Secant slope: $\frac{f(1) - f(0)}{1 - 0} = \frac{1 - 0}{1} = 1$.
   * **Result**: $f'(c) = 0 \neq 1$ for all $c \in (0, 1)$. The failure of continuity at even a single boundary point invalidates the guarantee.

Let $f(x) = x^2$ on $[a, b]$. Find the exact value of $c \in (a, b)$ guaranteed by <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" />. What geometric relationship does $c$ have with the interval endpoints?

Prove that the polynomial $p(x) = x^5 + 4x - 7$ has **exactly one** real root.
State explicitly which structural theorems guarantee (a) existence and (b) uniqueness.

<ExerciseSolution>
<p slot="hint" class="m-0">Use <Ref id="ivt" /> to establish existence on a compact interval, and <Ref id="rolles-theorem" /> (or sign of $p'$) to prove uniqueness by contradiction.</p>

**Part 1: Existence (via <Ref id="ivt" />)**
$p(x)$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
Evaluate at endpoints $x = 1$ and $x = 2$:
$$p(1) = 1^5 + 4(1) - 7 = 1 + 4 - 7 = -2 < 0.$$
$$p(2) = 2^5 + 4(2) - 7 = 32 + 8 - 7 = 33 > 0.$$
Since $p(1) < 0 < p(2)$, by the Intermediate Value Theorem (<Ref id="ivt" />), there exists at least one $r \in (1, 2)$ such that $p(r) = 0$.

**Part 2: Uniqueness (via <Ref id="rolles-theorem" />)**
Suppose for contradiction that $p(x)$ has two distinct real roots $r_1 < r_2$.
Then $p(r_1) = p(r_2) = 0$.
Since $p$ is continuous on $[r_1, r_2]$ and differentiable on $(r_1, r_2)$, by Rolle's Theorem (<Ref id="rolles-theorem" />), there must exist some $c \in (r_1, r_2)$ such that $p'(c) = 0$.
Now compute the derivative:
$$p'(x) = 5x^4 + 4.$$
Since $x^4 \ge 0$ for all real $x$, $5x^4 + 4 \ge 4 > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
Thus $p'(x)$ is strictly positive everywhere and can never equal $0$.
This contradicts the existence of $c$.
Therefore, $p(x)$ cannot have two or more real roots; the root is strictly unique.

<Exercise id="ex-inequality-mvt" type="prove" level="synthesis" title="Proving Functional Inequalities: Sine Bound">

Use <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" /> to prove that $\sin x < x$ for all $x > 0$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Apply the Mean Value Theorem to $f(t) = \sin t$ on the interval $[0, x]$ for $x > 0$.">

1. Let $x > 0$. Consider $f(t) = \sin t$ on the closed interval $[0, x]$.
2. $f$ is continuous on $[0, x]$ and differentiable on $(0, x)$ with $f'(t) = \cos t$.
3. By <Ref id="mean-value-theorem" />, there exists $c \in (0, x)$ such that:
   $$\frac{\sin x - \sin 0}{x - 0} = \cos c \implies \frac{\sin x}{x} = \cos c.$$
4. For any $c \in (0, x)$, we know that $\cos c \le 1$.
   Furthermore, since $c \in (0, x)$ and $c > 0$, $\cos c = 1$ can only occur if $c$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$. But at $c = 0$, $\cos 0 = 1$; for all $c \in (0, 2\pi)$, $\cos c < 1$.
   In general, for any $c > 0$, the inequality $\cos c < 1$ holds almost everywhere, and specifically on $(0, \min(x, 2\pi))$, $\cos c < 1$.
   For $x \ge 1$: $\sin x \le 1 \le x$, and since $\sin x \le 1 < x$ for $x > 1$, the claim holds.
   For $0 < x \le 1$: $0 < c < x \le 1 < \pi/2$, where cosine is strictly decreasing from $1$, so $\cos c < \cos 0 = 1$.
5. Therefore:
   $$\frac{\sin x}{x} = \cos c < 1 \implies \sin x < x.$$

<Exercise id="ex-cauchy-mvt-ratio" type="construct" level="application" title="Cauchy MVT Ratio Evaluation">

Let $f(x) = x^3$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 1$ on $[1, 3]$. Find the exact value of $c \in (1, 3)$ guaranteed by <Ref id="cauchy-mvt" />.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Compute the ratio $\frac{f(3)-f(1)}{g(3)-g(1)}$ and set it equal to $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$.">

1. Compute function values:
   $f(3) = 27, f(1) = 1 \implies f(3) - f(1) = 26$.
   $g(3) = 10, g(1) = 2 \implies g(3) - g(1) = 8$.
2. The secant ratio is:
   $$\frac{f(3) - f(1)}{g(3) - g(1)} = \frac{26}{8} = \frac{13}{4}.$$
3. The derivatives are $f'(x) = 3x^2$ and $g'(x) = 2x$.
   Note $g'(x) = 2x \ne 0$ on $(1, 3)$.
4. By <Ref id="cauchy-mvt" />, set:
   $$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{3c^2}{2c} = \frac{3c}{2} = \frac{13}{4}.$$
5. Solve for $c$:
   $$3c = \frac{13}{2} \implies c = \frac{13}{6} \approx 2.167.$$
   Since $1 < \frac{13}{6} < 3$, $c \in (1, 3)$ as guaranteed.

1. **Step 1 (Check initial form):**

   The form is indeterminate $0/0$.
2. **Apply <Ref id="lhopital-rule" /> once:**
   $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{2x}.$$
3. **Step 2 (Check second form):**
   $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin x = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} 2x = 0$.

   $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{2x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{2}.$$

   $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{2} = \frac{\cos 0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}.$$
