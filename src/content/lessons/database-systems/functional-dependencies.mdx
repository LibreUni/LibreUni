# Functional dependencies: state what determines a fact

A functional dependency is a design claim: once you know one fact, another fact is forced. It is not an observation that happened to hold in three rows of a spreadsheet. That distinction lets us ask whether a relation stores one fact once or repeats it under many keys.

<MathStatement id="fd-definition" kind="definition" title="Functional dependency">

For a relation schema $R$ and attribute sets $X, Y \subseteq R$, the functional dependency

$$
X \to Y
$$

holds when every **legal** instance of $R$ has this property: any two tuples that agree on $X$ also agree on $Y$. $X$ is the determinant; $Y$ is determined by it.
</MathStatement>

{/* Source tracking: Functional-dependency definitions, closure, and normal-form use are synthesized from the schema-refinement chapters of Ramakrishnan and Gehrke, *Database Management Systems*. */}

## Begin with a domain rule, not a table pattern

> **Definition.** A domain rule describes which future instances are legal. It is the evidence for a functional dependency; a repeated pattern in a current export is not.

Assume a university has exactly one instructor and one room for each scheduled section identified by `(course_code, section, term)`.

$$
(course\_code, section, term) \to (instructor, room)
$$

> **Worked example.** In `Section(course_code, section, term, instructor, room)`, the stated scheduling rule makes `(course_code, section, term)` determine `instructor` and `room`. It does **not** say that `room` determines `instructor`: different sections can use the same room with different instructors.

> **Counterexample.** A sample in which every row has a different `student_email` does not prove $student\_email \to student\_id$. The address may be missing, reused, or changeable unless the institution declares and enforces it as a unique identifier. Dependencies come from the legal domain, ideally backed by a key or explicit policy.

### Prediction: spot the unsupported dependency

The first three rows of a relation all show `term = '2026S'` and `instructor = 'Ng'`. May you infer $term \to instructor$? What extra domain rule would make it valid?

<ExerciseSolution hint="A dependency must survive every future legal row, not merely the current sample.">

No. Another course can be taught by another instructor in the same term. The dependency would be valid only under a much stronger rule such as “the institution has exactly one instructor for every course in every term,” which is not the intended domain.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Compute closure as a trace

Attribute closure answers: starting with attributes $X$, what attributes can the dependency set force? Let

$$
F = \{A \to B,\; B \to C,\; CD \to E\}.
$$

To calculate $AD^+$, begin with $\{A,D\}$ and add only a right side whose left side is already present.

| Step | Available attributes | Dependency enabled | New attribute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | $A,D$ | — | — |
| 1 | $A,D$ | $A \to B$ | $B$ |
| 2 | $A,B,D$ | $B \to C$ | $C$ |
| 3 | $A,B,C,D$ | $CD \to E$ | $E$ |

So $AD^+ = \{A,B,C,D,E\}$. The order of enabled steps can vary; the final closure cannot, provided you keep applying dependencies until none adds an attribute.

<MathProof id="fd-closure-trace" for="fd-definition" title="Why the trace is justified" collapsible>

Each step preserves equality on the starting attributes. If two tuples agree on `A`, $A \to B$ forces agreement on `B`; agreement on `B` forces `C`; agreement on both `C` and `D` forces `E`. Thus any two tuples agreeing on `A,D` agree on every attribute in the closure.
</MathProof>

### Try the closure tool

Choose seed attributes, fire each dependency only when its determinant is available, and compare your trace to the displayed closure. Use `A,D` after trying a smaller seed such as `A`.

> **Invariant.** $X$ is a superkey for schema $R$ exactly when $X^+$ contains every attribute of $R$ under the assumed dependency set. It is a candidate key only if no proper subset of $X$ is also a superkey.

### Apply closure to a real key question

Let `Registration(student_id, course_code, term, instructor, room)` have the dependency

$$
(course\_code, term) \to (instructor, room).
$$

Is `(student_id, course_code, term)` a candidate key under this dependency set? Show the closure and the minimality check.

<ExerciseSolution hint="The proposed attributes already include three columns; use the dependency to add the remaining two, then try removing one attribute at a time.">

Its closure starts with `student_id, course_code, term` and adds `instructor, room`, so it is a superkey. Removing `student_id` cannot identify which student's registration is meant. Removing `course_code` or `term` prevents the given dependency from identifying the scheduled section. Under the stated dependency set, it is a candidate key. A different domain rule, such as a separate registration ID, can produce an additional candidate key.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Derive dependencies without hand-waving

Three sound inference rules are enough for many small derivations.

| Rule | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reflexivity | If $Y \subseteq X$, then $X \to Y$ | $AB \to A$ |
| Augmentation | If $X \to Y$, then $XZ \to YZ$ | $A \to B$ implies $AD \to BD$ |
| Transitivity | If $X \to Y$ and $Y \to Z$, then $X \to Z$ | $A \to B$, $B \to C$ implies $A \to C$ |

> **Worked example.** From $A \to B$ and $B \to C$, derive $A \to C$ by transitivity. With $A \to B$, derive $AD \to BD$ by augmentation. In the earlier closure, these steps explain why `A,D` can eventually enable $CD \to E$—first it gains `B`, then `C`.

> **Warning.** Do not reverse a dependency. $course\_code \to title$ does not imply $title \to course\_code$ unless the domain makes titles unique. Direction records a claim about equality, not a visual arrow between columns.

### Check a derivation

Given $F = \{A \to B, B \to C, CD \to E\}$, can $D \to E$ be derived? Can $AD \to E$ be derived?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Compute the two closures separately. A dependency can fire only when all determinant attributes are available.">

$D^+ = \{D\}$, so $D \to E$ cannot be derived. $AD^+$ gains `B` from $A \to B$, then `C` from $B \to C$, so it contains `C,D` and gains `E` from $CD \to E$. Therefore $AD \to E$ is derivable.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Reduce a dependency set before using it

A minimal cover gives a compact equivalent set of dependencies. It is a tool for reasoning, not a claim that the compact version is easier for every reader to understand.

> **Example.** Start with

$$
F = \{A \to BC,\; B \to C,\; AB \to C\}.
$$

Split the right side: $A \to B$ and $A \to C$. Then $A \to C$ follows from $A \to B$ and $B \to C$, so remove it. `AB → C` follows from $B \to C$ because `B` is already part of `AB`, so remove it too. A minimal cover is $\{A \to B, B \to C\}$.

### Transfer task

The scheduling system adds multiple meeting times to a section. It currently claims

$$
(course\_code, section, term) \to room.
$$

Give a legal scenario that breaks this dependency. Then redesign the relation so one row has a clear grain and state one valid dependency for the revised design.

<ExerciseSolution hint="A section can now meet in two rooms or at two times. Put the repeated meeting fact in its own relation.">

One section can meet Monday in `R101` and Wednesday in `Lab3`; both rows share `(course_code, section, term)` but have different rooms, so the old dependency fails. Use `Meeting(course_code, section, term, meeting_time, room)` with grain “one scheduled meeting of one section.” If each meeting time for a section has exactly one room, then $(course\_code, section, term, meeting\_time) \to room$ is valid. Validate that rule with the scheduling policy, not just a sample export.
</ExerciseSolution>
