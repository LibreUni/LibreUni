# Normalization: decompose without losing facts

Normalization is not a ritual of splitting tables. It asks whether one tuple stores several facts whose values can change independently. If it does, a dependency tells us where the repetition comes from and a lossless decomposition tells us whether we can separate the facts safely.

> **Definition.** A relation has an update anomaly when one logical fact is stored in multiple tuples and those copies can disagree. Normalization uses dependencies to move each independently determined fact to a relation whose key actually determines it.

{/* Source tracking: The normal-form definitions, lossless-join criterion, and decomposition trade-offs are synthesized from Ramakrishnan and Gehrke’s schema-refinement chapters. */}

## See the repeated fact before naming a normal form

Suppose one relation records both registrations and scheduled-section facts:

```text
Registration(student_id, course_code, term, instructor, room)
```

The grain is **one student's registration in one course offering during one term**. The scheduling rule says

$$
(course\_code, term) \to (instructor, room).
$$

| `student_id` | `course_code` | `term` | `instructor` | `room` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ada | DB101 | 2026S | Ng | R101 |
| Bo | DB101 | 2026S | Ng | R101 |
| Cy | DB201 | 2026S | Shah | Lab3 |

> **Worked example.** `(student_id, course_code, term)` is a candidate key under the stated rules, yet `instructor` and `room` depend only on `(course_code, term)`. Changing DB101's room requires editing Ada's and Bo's rows. A missed edit creates a contradiction about the same offering.

### Predict the anomaly

DB101 moves from R101 to R202 before any student enrolls. Which operation is awkward in this relation, and what data could be lost if the last DB101 registration is deleted?

<ExerciseSolution hint="The relation's grain begins with a student registration, but the new fact is about an offering.">

Inserting the offering fact is awkward because there is no `student_id` to make a registration tuple. Deleting the last DB101 registration also deletes the only stored instructor/room fact. These are insertion and deletion anomalies; they reveal that an offering fact has been forced into a registration-grain relation.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Use the normal forms as tests

| Form | Useful test |
| --- | --- |
| 1NF | Each attribute value is modeled as one value in its chosen domain; repeating groups are made explicit relations when they represent repeated facts. |
| 2NF | Every non-prime attribute is fully dependent on every candidate key; no non-prime fact depends on a proper subset of a composite candidate key. |
| 3NF | For every nontrivial $X \to A$, either $X$ is a superkey or $A$ is prime (belongs to a candidate key). |
| BCNF | For every nontrivial $X \to Y$, $X$ is a superkey. |

> **Warning.** “Atomic” does not mean “never split a value.” A postal address may be a single value for one application and several independently queried facts for another. Choose domains from the operations and constraints you must support.

In `Registration`, `(course_code, term)` is not a superkey because it does not determine `student_id`. Its dependency to `instructor, room` therefore violates BCNF. Because those attributes are non-prime and depend on a proper subset of the composite key, it also violates 2NF.

### Recheck the determinant

Trace the dependency inside the actual normalization example rather than changing fixtures.

| Start | Dependency applied | Closure after the step | Missing attribute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `(course_code, term)` | `(course_code, term) → (instructor, room)` | `(course_code, term, instructor, room)` | `student_id` |

<ExerciseSolution hint="In `Registration`, the scheduling determinant cannot add a student identifier.">

For `Registration`, `(course_code, term)^+` adds `instructor` and `room`, not `student_id`; it is not a superkey. That is exactly the BCNF failure. The functional-dependencies lesson's explorer is the place to practice the general closure algorithm; this table keeps the proof tied to the relation being decomposed.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Decompose, then prove the join is lossless

Split the relation on the violating dependency:

```text
Offering(course_code, term, instructor, room)
Enrollment(student_id, course_code, term)
```

`Offering` has one tuple per scheduled offering. `Enrollment` has one tuple per student's registration in that offering. A foreign key from `(course_code, term)` in `Enrollment` to the key of `Offering` records the intended connection.

<MathStatement id="lossless-binary" kind="proposition" title="Binary lossless-join criterion">

For a decomposition of relation $R$ into $R_1$ and $R_2$, the decomposition is lossless with respect to $F$ when

$$
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1
\quad \text{or} \quad
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2
$$

is implied by $F^+$. In words: the shared attributes determine one whole side.
</MathStatement>

For every proposed split, submit a three-part evidence record: the two projected relations, their natural join on the shared attributes, and either (a) equality with the original legal instance under the stated dependencies or (b) a concrete spurious tuple. A lossless claim without the shared-attribute closure and this counterexample test is incomplete. The join test is a finite witness for the supplied instance; the criterion above is the dependency-level argument that generalizes it.

> **Worked example.** The shared attributes are `(course_code, term)`. They determine all of `Offering` by the scheduling dependency, so joining `Enrollment` back to `Offering` does not invent extra registration/offering combinations. The decomposition is lossless under the stated rule.

> **Counterexample.** Let $R(A,B,C)$ contain `(a1,b1,c1)` and `(a2,b1,c2)`. Decompose it into $R_1(A,B)$ and $R_2(B,C)$ without any dependency that makes `B` determine either side. Joining the projections produces four tuples, including spurious `(a1,b1,c2)` and `(a2,b1,c1)`. Splitting columns alone is not a proof.

### Work the criterion

For the `Registration` split, name $R_1$, $R_2$, their intersection, and the dependency that satisfies the criterion.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Put the scheduled-offering relation on the side determined by the shared pair.">

Take $R_1 = Offering(course\_code, term, instructor, room)$ and $R_2 = Enrollment(student\_id, course\_code, term)$. Their intersection is $(course\_code, term)$, and $(course\_code, term) \to R_1$ follows from $(course\_code, term) \to (instructor, room)$ plus reflexivity. The criterion therefore proves the binary decomposition lossless.
</ExerciseSolution>

## BCNF is not the only goal

To decompose a schema $S$ on a BCNF-violating dependency $X \to Y$, form:

$$
S_1 = X \cup Y, \qquad S_2 = S - (Y - X).
$$

> **Definition.** A BCNF decomposition keeps the determinant $X$ in its second relation. Writing only $S - Y$ can accidentally remove attributes needed to join the pieces back and is not the BCNF decomposition rule.

BCNF removes every dependency whose determinant is not a superkey. It can sacrifice local enforcement of another dependency. 3NF is often chosen when lossless decomposition **and** dependency preservation matter more than removing every BCNF anomaly.

> **Worked example.** Let $R(A,B,C)$ have $F = \{AB \to C, C \to B\}$. Candidate keys are `AB` and `AC`; all attributes are prime, so $C \to B$ satisfies 3NF but violates BCNF because `C` is not a superkey. Decomposing on $C \to B$ yields `CB` and `AC`. The original dependency $AB \to C$ is not contained in either relation, so enforcing it may require a join or another design decision.

### Choose the trade-off

Your team can enforce dependencies with single-relation checks during every write, but a BCNF decomposition would require checking one dependency across a join. What design question should be documented before choosing 3NF or BCNF?

<ExerciseSolution hint="There is no automatic winner: name the anomaly risk and the cost of enforcement.">

Document the business cost of inconsistent redundant facts, the dependency that would no longer be locally enforceable under BCNF, the write path that must enforce it, and the expected query/join cost. Choose 3NF when preserving that dependency locally is important and its residual redundancy is understood; choose BCNF when the stronger anomaly protection outweighs that enforcement cost.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Transfer task: normalize a revised schedule

The university permits a section to meet in several rooms at several times. Start from

```text
SectionRegistration(student_id, course_code, section, term, meeting_time, room, instructor)
```

Assume `(course_code, section, term) → instructor` and `(course_code, section, term, meeting_time) → room`. Propose relations with clear grains, state one key for each, and show one binary lossless-join argument. Do not invent a dependency between `room` and `instructor`.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Separate registration, section, and repeated meeting facts. Each relation should store one kind of fact once.">

One design is `Section(course_code, section, term, instructor)`, `Meeting(course_code, section, term, meeting_time, room)`, and `Registration(student_id, course_code, section, term)`. Their grains are one section, one scheduled meeting, and one student registration. Keys can be `(course_code, section, term)`, `(course_code, section, term, meeting_time)`, and `(student_id, course_code, section, term)`. Decomposing `SectionRegistration` first into `Section` and the remaining attributes is lossless because the shared `(course_code, section, term)` determines all of `Section`; apply the same check for the `Meeting` split using its stated determinant.
</ExerciseSolution>
