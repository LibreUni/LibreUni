Normalization is not a ritual of splitting tables. It asks whether one tuple stores several facts whose values can change independently. If it does, a dependency tells us where the repetition comes from and a lossless decomposition tells us whether we can separate the facts safely.

> **Definition.** A relation has an update anomaly when one logical fact is stored in multiple tuples and those copies can disagree. Normalization uses dependencies to move each independently determined fact to a relation whose key actually determines it.

## See the repeated fact before naming a normal form

Suppose one relation records both scheduled tasks and cluster deployment facts:

```text
TaskAllocation(task_id, service_name, cluster_id, node_ip, port)
```

The grain is **one task execution assigned to a deployed service on a cluster**. The infrastructure scheduling rule says

$$
(service\_name, cluster\_id) \to (node\_ip, port).
$$

| `task_id` | `service_name` | `cluster_id` | `node_ip` | `port` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| t-101 | auth-svc | us-east-1 | 10.0.1.5 | 8080 |
| t-102 | auth-svc | us-east-1 | 10.0.1.5 | 8080 |
| t-103 | billing-svc | us-east-1 | 10.0.1.12 | 8443 |

> **Worked example.** `(task_id, service_name, cluster_id)` is a candidate key under the stated rules, yet `node_ip` and `port` depend only on `(service_name, cluster_id)`. Moving `auth-svc` to another node requires editing rows `t-101` and `t-102`. A missed edit creates an inconsistent state about the same service deployment.

### Predict the anomaly

`auth-svc` migrates to node `10.0.1.9` before any task is scheduled. Which operation is awkward in this relation, and what data could be lost if the last `billing-svc` task finishes and its row is deleted?

<ExerciseSolution hint="The relation's grain begins with a task execution, but the deployment fact is about a cluster service.">

Inserting the service deployment is awkward because there is no `task_id` to form a legal tuple. Deleting the last `billing-svc` task also deletes the only record of its assigned node and port. These are insertion and deletion anomalies; they reveal that a service deployment fact has been forced into a task-grain relation.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Use the normal forms as tests

| Form | Useful test |
| --- | --- |
| 1NF | Each attribute value is modeled as one value in its chosen domain; repeating groups are made explicit relations when they represent repeated facts. |
| 2NF | Every non-prime attribute is fully dependent on every candidate key; no non-prime fact depends on a proper subset of a composite candidate key. |
| 3NF | For every nontrivial $X \to A$, either $X$ is a superkey or $A$ is prime (belongs to a candidate key). |
| BCNF | For every nontrivial $X \to Y$, $X$ is a superkey. |

> **Warning.** “Atomic” does not mean “never split a value.” A postal address may be a single value for one application and several independently queried facts for another. Choose domains from the operations and constraints you must support.

In `TaskAllocation`, `(service_name, cluster_id)` is not a superkey because it does not determine `task_id`. Its dependency to `node_ip, port` therefore violates BCNF. Because those attributes are non-prime and depend on a proper subset of the composite key, it also violates 2NF.

Trace the dependency inside the actual normalization example rather than changing fixtures.

| Start | Dependency applied | Closure after the step | Missing attribute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `(service_name, cluster_id)` | `(service_name, cluster_id) → (node_ip, port)` | `(service_name, cluster_id, node_ip, port)` | `task_id` |

<ExerciseSolution hint="In `TaskAllocation`, the service deployment determinant cannot add a task identifier.">

For `TaskAllocation`, `(service_name, cluster_id)^+` adds `node_ip` and `port`, not `task_id`; it is not a superkey. That is exactly the BCNF failure. The functional-dependencies lesson's explorer is the place to practice the general closure algorithm; this table keeps the proof tied to the relation being decomposed.

## Decompose, then prove the join is lossless

Split the relation on the violating dependency:

```text
ServiceDeployment(service_name, cluster_id, node_ip, port)
TaskAssignment(task_id, service_name, cluster_id)
```

`ServiceDeployment` has one tuple per service deployed to a cluster. `TaskAssignment` has one tuple per task scheduled on that deployment. A foreign key from `(service_name, cluster_id)` in `TaskAssignment` to the key of `ServiceDeployment` records the intended connection.

<MathStatement id="lossless-binary" kind="proposition" title="Binary lossless-join criterion">

For a decomposition of relation $R$ into $R_1$ and $R_2$, the decomposition is lossless with respect to $F$ when

$$
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1
\quad \text{or} \quad
(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2
$$

is implied by $F^+$. In words: the shared attributes determine one whole side.
</MathStatement>

For every proposed split, verify a three-part evidence record: the two projected relations, their natural join on the shared attributes, and either (a) equality with the original legal instance under the stated dependencies or (b) a concrete spurious tuple. A lossless claim without the shared-attribute closure and this counterexample test is incomplete. The join test is a finite witness for the supplied instance; the criterion above is the dependency-level argument that generalizes it.

> **Worked example.** The shared attributes are `(service_name, cluster_id)`. They determine all of `ServiceDeployment` by the deployment dependency `(service_name, cluster_id) -> (node_ip, port)`, so joining `TaskAssignment` back to `ServiceDeployment` does not invent extra task/deployment combinations. The decomposition is lossless under the stated rule.

> **Counterexample.** Let $R(A,B,C)$ contain `(a1,b1,c1)` and `(a2,b1,c2)`. Decompose it into $R_1(A,B)$ and $R_2(B,C)$ without any dependency that makes `B` determine either side. Joining the projections produces four tuples, including spurious `(a1,b1,c2)` and `(a2,b1,c1)`. Splitting columns alone is not a proof.

### Work the criterion

For the `TaskAllocation` split, name $R_1$, $R_2$, their intersection, and the dependency that satisfies the criterion.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Put the service-deployment relation on the side determined by the shared pair.">

Take $R_1 = ServiceDeployment(service\_name, cluster\_id, node\_ip, port)$ and $R_2 = TaskAssignment(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id)$. Their intersection is $(service\_name, cluster\_id)$, and $(service\_name, cluster\_id) \to R_1$ follows from $(service\_name, cluster\_id) \to (node\_ip, port)$ plus reflexivity. ## BCNF is not the only goal

To decompose a schema $S$ on a BCNF-violating dependency $X \to Y$, form:

$$
S_1 = X \cup Y, \qquad S_2 = S - (Y - X).
$$

> **Definition.** A BCNF decomposition keeps the determinant $X$ in its second relation. Writing only $S - Y$ can accidentally remove attributes needed to join the pieces back and is not the BCNF decomposition rule.

BCNF removes every dependency whose determinant is not a superkey. It can sacrifice local enforcement of another dependency. 3NF is often chosen when lossless decomposition **and** dependency preservation matter more than removing every BCNF anomaly.

> **Worked example.** Let $R(A,B,C)$ have $F = \{AB \to C, C \to B\}$. Candidate keys are `AB` and `AC`; all attributes are prime, so $C \to B$ satisfies 3NF but violates BCNF because `C` is not a superkey. Decomposing on $C \to B$ yields `CB` and `AC`. The original dependency $AB \to C$ is not contained in either relation, so enforcing it may require a join or another design decision.

### Choose the trade-off

Your team can enforce dependencies with single-relation checks during every write, but a BCNF decomposition would require checking one dependency across a join. What design question should be documented before choosing 3NF or BCNF?

<Exercise id="ex-norm-lossless-verification" type="verify" level="application" title="Binary Decomposition Losslessness Verification">

A relation schema $TaskAllocation(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id, node\_ip, port)$ is decomposed into $ServiceDeployment(service\_name, cluster\_id, node\_ip, port)$ and $TaskAssignment(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id)$. Using <Ref id="lossless-binary" />, verify whether this decomposition is lossless under functional dependency $(service\_name, cluster\_id) \to (node\_ip, port)$.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Find the shared attributes between ServiceDeployment and TaskAssignment, then compute their attribute closure.">

The intersection of attributes is $ServiceDeployment \cap TaskAssignment = \{service\_name, cluster\_id\}$. Under the given functional dependency, $\{service\_name, cluster\_id\}^+ = \{service\_name, cluster\_id, node\_ip, port\}$, which equals the entire attribute set of $ServiceDeployment$. By the binary lossless-join criterion, because the intersection functionally determines one of the decomposed relations, the decomposition is guaranteed to be lossless.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-norm-spurious-counterexample" type="construct" level="application" title="Spurious Tuple Construction on Arbitrary Split">

Let $R(A, B, C)$ be decomposed into $R_1(A, B)$ and $R_2(B, C)$ under empty dependency set $F = \emptyset$. Construct a concrete 2-tuple instance of $R$ whose natural join $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ produces spurious tuples, and identify the spurious combinations.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Choose two tuples that share a B value but have distinct A and C values.">

Let $R = \{(a_1, b_1, c_1), (a_2, b_1, c_2)\}$. The projections are $R_1 = \{(a_1, b_1), (a_2, b_1)\}$ and $R_2 = \{(b_1, c_1), (b_1, c_2)\}$. Computing $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ yields four tuples: $\{(a_1, b_1, c_1), (a_1, b_1, c_2), (a_2, b_1, c_1), (a_2, b_1, c_2)\}$. The tuples $(a_1, b_1, c_2)$ and $(a_2, b_1, c_1)$ are spurious tuples not present in $R$.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-norm-3nf-vs-bcnf" type="describe" level="recall" title="BCNF vs. 3NF Architectural Trade-Off Analysis">

Explain why schema $Address(Street, City, Zip)$ with dependencies $(Street, City) \to Zip$ and $Zip \to City$ is in 3NF but not BCNF. What engineering trade-off is made when deciding whether to decompose to BCNF?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Check candidate keys and whether every determinant is a superkey or every determined attribute is prime.">

Candidate keys are $(Street, City)$ and $(Street, Zip)$. In $Zip \to City$, $Zip$ is not a superkey, violating BCNF. However, $City$ is part of candidate key $(Street, City)$ (i.e. prime), so it satisfies 3NF. Decomposing to BCNF produces $(Zip, City)$ and $(Street, Zip)$, eliminating redundancy but destroying dependency preservation: enforcing $(Street, City) \to Zip$ now requires an expensive join assertion across both tables.

</ExerciseSolution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="ex-norm-multi-meeting-transfer" type="generalise" level="transfer" title="Lossless Normalization of Multi-Region Service Deployments">

A microservice deployment schema is given by $RegionalDeployment(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id, region, endpoint\_port, node\_ip)$ with dependencies $(service\_name, cluster\_id) \to node\_ip$ and $(service\_name, cluster\_id, region) \to endpoint\_port$. Propose a BCNF decomposition into three relations with stated grains, candidate keys, and lossless join justifications.

<ExerciseSolution hint="Separate service placement facts, regional routing endpoint facts, and task executions iteratively.">

Decompose into three relations:
1. $ServiceHost(service\_name, cluster\_id, node\_ip)$ with key $(service\_name, cluster\_id)$ and grain 'one node assignment for a service cluster'.
2. $RegionalIngress(service\_name, cluster\_id, region, endpoint\_port)$ with key $(service\_name, cluster\_id, region)$ and grain 'one regional ingress port for a cluster service'.
3. $TaskExecution(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id, region)$ with key $(task\_id, service\_name, cluster\_id, region)$ and grain 'one task execution allocated to a regional service'.
Each step is lossless: $ServiceHost$ intersects the remainder on $(service\_name, cluster\_id)$, which determines $ServiceHost$; $RegionalIngress$ intersects $TaskExecution$ on $(service\_name, cluster\_id, region)$, which is a candidate key of $RegionalIngress$.

</ExerciseSolution>
