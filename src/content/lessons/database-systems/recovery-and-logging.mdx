A commit response is a promise about a failure boundary. If a power loss follows an acknowledged order but precedes the page write, the order must still be recoverable. If a data page reaches storage before the log record explaining it, recovery loses its evidence. Logging is therefore a protocol for reconstructing a legal state, not an audit trail added after the fact.

## A durable log prefix orders safe writes

> **Invariant.** Before a changed data page reaches durable storage, the log records describing that page change must be durable. Before a client receives a durable-commit acknowledgement, the commit record must be durable under the stated configuration.

| LSN | Transaction | Record | Durable before crash? |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 900 | T1 | begin | yes |
| 901 | T1 | set stock 20 → 13 | yes |
| 902 | T1 | commit | yes |
| 903 | T2 | begin | yes |
| 904 | T2 | set stock 13 → 6 | yes |
| 905 | T2 | commit | no |

The stable log prefix ending at 904 supports T1's commit. A crash before 905 becomes durable cannot honestly expose T2 as committed. A data page may contain an update before its transaction commits; recovery's transaction status decides whether to preserve, undo, or hide it. Page LSNs make redo idempotent: if a page already reflects LSN 901 or later, reapplying 901 is unnecessary.

### Prediction — a page beats its log

A page reflecting LSN 911 reaches storage, but the log is durable only through 910. Why is this STEAL-without-WAL failure mode an error even if the update happens to be correct?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Recovery needs a durable explanation for every durable page effect.">
The durable page contains an effect whose redo/undo record may be absent. After a crash, recovery cannot determine whether the change belonged to a committed transaction, reconstruct an overwritten older value, or repeat the operation safely on another page. WAL forbids this ordering; correctness is not established by observing one lucky final value.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Redo repeats history; undo removes losers

> **Definition.** A **winner** has a durable commit record at crash time. A **loser** began but lacks a durable commit. Recovery repeats durable changes that are missing from pages and removes or hides loser effects according to its log and visibility design.

### Worked trace — crash after page flush

T1 writes stock 20 → 13 at LSN 901 and commits at 902. The page flushed at LSN 901 before the crash. T2's update at 904 may be present in the buffer, but its data-page effect has not reached durable storage; T2 has no durable commit. Recovery sees page LSN 901, skips redundant redo of 901, and starts from a state that includes T1 but not T2. If T2's update had reached the page, recovery would undo or make it invisible because T2 is a loser.

<ExerciseSolution title="Why is redo still needed when logs are durable?">

A committed log record can survive while its changed page does not. Redo brings persistent pages up to the durable committed history. Undo handles the opposite case: a page can contain a loser update that reached storage before the crash. Neither pass substitutes for the other.

</ExerciseSolution>

## ARIES makes undo repeatable

{/* Source tracking: Analysis, redo, undo, and compensation log records are synthesized from Mohan et al., ARIES (1992); the LSN trace is original. */}

The three-phase sketch above becomes a real recovery protocol only when an interrupted undo can itself survive a crash. ARIES adds **compensation log records** (CLRs) and a per-transaction **undo-next LSN** so that recovery never repeats an undo it already performed.

> **Definition.** A compensation log record (CLR) describes an undo action and carries an `undoNextLSN`, the next earlier record in that transaction's undo chain to examine. That record can be a begin record rather than another update. A CLR is redo-only: it can be replayed during redo but is not itself undone.

**Worked trace.** T2 begins at LSN 903, updates stock at 904, and crashes without a durable commit. During undo, recovery appends CLR 906 for the reversal with `undoNextLSN = 903` and applies the undo. WAL requires that CLR to be durable before its changed page is flushed. Suppose 906 is durable and recovery crashes again before finishing. On restart, redo can repeat 906 if its effect is missing from the page; undo resumes at 903 instead of undoing 904 again. If the CLR was not durable, recovery follows the durable log instead: WAL prevents the corresponding undo page effect from having been safely flushed first.

| Phase | Reads | Produces | Key invariant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Analysis | log from the last checkpoint | transaction table, dirty-page table | every loser is identified before any undo |
| Redo | log forward from the smallest dirty-page `recLSN` found during analysis | pages brought to their logged history | page LSN skips changes already present |
| Undo | losers' log records backward | CLRs and restored pre-images | `undoNextLSN` advances exactly once per action |

Analysis starts from the checkpoint designated by the master record and reconstructs transaction and dirty-page state. A fuzzy checkpoint records active transactions and dirty pages while work continues; the dirty-page table can contain a `recLSN` older than the checkpoint, so redo can start earlier. The correctness claim depends on WAL and the recovery protocol, not merely checkpoint frequency.

> **Warning.** A CLR is not an application-level compensating transaction. It repairs physical recovery state. Reversing a shipped order, a sent email, or a payment requires an idempotent business compensation and cannot be inferred from a database undo record.

## Recovery objectives require distinct artifacts

> **Warning.** A streaming replica improves failover capability; it is not automatically a historical backup. An accidental delete or logical corruption can replicate quickly to every replica.

| Artifact | Main question answered | Cannot replace |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WAL / transaction log | how to recover changes after a base state | a base copy once retention is exhausted |
| full or incremental backup | what durable base can be restored | recent changes without logs or later backups |
| replica | how quickly can service continue after a node loss | an independently retained historical restore point |
| checksum / invariant test | did restored bytes and domain facts survive? | the data copy itself |

A stated recovery point objective (RPO) is acceptable data loss, measured in time or transactions. A recovery time objective (RTO) is acceptable restoration time. They are claims until a restore drill measures them. A backup file existing in object storage is not evidence that it restores, replays logs, starts, and passes business invariants.

### Application — design a restore drill

The product claims RPO ≤ 5 minutes and RTO ≤ 60 minutes after a bad migration. State a drill that can disprove either number.

<ExerciseSolution>
Capture a known workload and timestamps, take the documented base backup, retain the required WAL/archive interval, then introduce the bad migration. Restore into an isolated environment to a target time no more than five minutes before the declared incident boundary. Measure elapsed time through startup and validation, compare row checksums where appropriate, and test domain invariants such as tenant ownership and nonnegative stock. Failure to reach the time target disproves RPO; exceeding the measured recovery budget disproves RTO.
</ExerciseSolution>

## Corruption and accidental change need different responses

> **Example.** A checksum failure on one page calls for diagnosis, a known-good recovery source, and evidence that copies are independent. An accidental DELETE with a valid checksum calls for point-in-time recovery or controlled logical repair; checksum success says nothing about intended content.

### Transfer — select the first safe action

A replica is healthy but has already replayed an accidental tenant-wide deletion. A nightly backup exists and WAL is retained. What is the first safe recovery plan?

<ExerciseSolution hint="Do not promote the replica merely because it is available; it has the same logical damage.">
Preserve the primary, replica, backup, and WAL evidence; restore the backup plus WAL to an isolated target before the deletion; verify the tenant scope and invariants; then choose a controlled logical export/import or a promotion plan with client fencing. The replica can reduce outage time in a node-loss case, but here it is not an independent recovery source.
</ExerciseSolution>
