# Storage Devices and I/O Scheduling

A scheduler sorts every block request by logical block address because that reduced seek distance on a magnetic disk. The same policy is deployed on an NVMe SSD. Requests from one latency-sensitive process wait behind a long adjacent run, while the SSD's independent internal channels remain underfilled. “Sequential is faster” was promoted from a device-specific observation to a universal rule.

Storage scheduling sits between workload intent and a device whose service function may be mechanical, solid-state, virtualized, or remote. A policy is defensible only when its cost model matches the medium and its objective includes more than aggregate throughput.

> **Media-aware scheduling contract.** Request order must be justified by measured or specified device behavior, queue semantics, fairness boundaries, and durability constraints. Logical address proximity alone is not a universal service-time metric.

## The block layer does not erase the medium

Applications and filesystems issue operations on logical byte or block ranges. The storage stack may merge, split, queue, prioritize, and dispatch them. A controller or device can reorder again, serve requests in parallel, cache writes, or translate logical addresses internally.

The observable latency of request $i$ can be decomposed as

$$
T_i=T_{queue,i}+T_{controller,i}+T_{medium,i}+T_{transfer,i}+T_{completion,i}.
$$

The decomposition prevents one useful optimization—such as reducing seek—from being mistaken for the entire response time.

**Retrieval — identify hidden reordering.** A filesystem submits writes A then B, and the driver rings one queue doorbell. Name two lower layers that may still reorder work and the interface feature needed when A must become durable before B.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the diagnostic answer</summary>

Controller firmware and the medium's internal scheduler/translation layer may reorder. The host needs an ordering/durability mechanism defined by the device protocol, such as an ordered dependency, flush, or force-unit-access semantics as appropriate. Submission order alone is not a durability proof. Exact commands are protocol-specific and are outside the assessed filesystem-recovery scope.

## Magnetic disks pay mechanical positioning cost

A simplified magnetic-disk access has

$$
T_{medium}=T_{seek}+T_{rotation},
$$

followed by transfer. Seek moves the actuator to the target track/cylinder; rotational latency waits for the desired sector to pass under the head. With rotation rate $R$ revolutions/minute and uniformly distributed angular position,

$$
E[T_{rotation}]=\frac{1}{2}\frac{60}{R}\text{ seconds}.
$$

At 7,200 RPM, one revolution is 8.33 ms and average rotational latency under the uniform-angle model is 4.17 ms.

{/* Source tracking: Mechanical seek and rotation definitions were checked against Seagate magnetic-drive product manuals; numeric examples below are declared teaching assumptions, not claims about a current product. */}

### Worked service-time estimate

Assume a magnetic disk with measured 8 ms seek for a particular distance, 7,200 RPM, sustained transfer 200 MB/s, no queueing, and no controller-cache hit. A 64 KiB request has

$$
T_{transfer}=\frac{65{,}536}{200{,}000{,}000}\text{ s}=0.328\text{ ms}.
$$

The estimate is

$$
8+4.17+0.328=12.498\text{ ms}.
$$

This is not a constant per-request law. Seek is nonlinear in distance, rotational position is not always uniform, adjacent sectors can stream, controller caches can satisfy a request, and zone geometry changes transfer rates.

### Cylinder movement is a proxy, not time

Classical scheduling traces use total cylinder movement because nearby requests often reduce mechanical positioning. For initial head 53 and queue

```text
98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67
```

the supplied model produces:

| Policy | Service path | Movement |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| FCFS | `53→98→183→37→122→14→124→65→67` | 640 cylinders |
| SSTF | `53→65→67→37→14→98→122→124→183` | 236 cylinders |
| SCAN upward | `53→65→67→98→122→124→183→199→37→14` | 331 cylinders |

SSTF wins this movement total, but repeatedly arriving nearby requests can postpone a distant request indefinitely. SCAN trades some movement for directional progress. The model's SCAN travels to cylinder 199 before reversing; LOOK would reverse at the final request in the direction, producing a different path.

## FCFS, SSTF, SCAN, and deadlines optimize different contracts

| Policy | Useful property | Failure boundary | Repair direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FCFS | transparent arrival order | long seeks and convoy effects | merge/batch without erasing age |
| SSTF | low next seek under cylinder proxy | starvation of distant requests | age or deadline override |
| SCAN | directional progress | endpoint travel; unequal waiting by position | LOOK/C-SCAN or deadlines |
| deadline hybrid | limits age while batching locality | tuning couples throughput and latency | measure read/write classes and tails |

A deadline scheduler can keep requests in both address order and age order. It dispatches batches by address for locality but checks whether an old request must be served. The guarantee is only as strong as the precise timer, batch, and lower-layer assumptions. Linux's documented deadline scheduler, for example, exposes separate expiration and batch controls rather than claiming exact completion deadlines.

{/* Source tracking: Deadline batching and expiration behavior were verified against Linux kernel deadline I/O scheduler documentation. */}

### Worked policy decision: interactive reads and bulk writes

A magnetic drive receives 4 KiB interactive reads with a 20 ms start-service objective and 1 MiB sequential background writes. Pure FCFS lets a long write run delay reads. Pure read priority can starve writeback and eventually block the filesystem. A hybrid may:

- merge adjacent writes into bounded batches;
- dispatch reads preferentially;
- force any request whose age exceeds its class threshold;
- cap consecutive reads so dirty writeback progresses; and
- measure **start-service** and completion separately.

The design is incomplete without queue-depth and arrival assumptions. A deadline cannot be guaranteed if admitted work exceeds device capacity and no request can be rejected or degraded.

## SSDs replace a head with translation and parallelism

NAND flash cannot overwrite a programmed page in place. A flash translation layer (FTL) maps host logical blocks to physical flash pages, writes updates out of place, and reclaims erase blocks through garbage collection. Thus consecutive logical addresses need not remain physically adjacent, and background firmware work can produce variable latency.

{/* Source tracking: FTL logical-to-physical remapping, out-of-place updates, and garbage collection were verified against Kim et al.'s BPLRU primary paper and related USENIX flash-storage research. */}

An NVMe interface exposes memory-based submission and completion queues and supports substantial parallelism. Host software can map queues to CPUs and keep multiple commands outstanding. The important scheduling dimensions shift toward:

- enough queue depth to expose device parallelism without unbounded waiting;
- avoiding one global software lock or queue bottleneck;
- read/write and latency-class isolation;
- merge/split overhead and transfer size;
- firmware garbage-collection tail latency;
- flush/order semantics; and
- fairness across processes, cgroups, or virtual machines.

Linux's blk-mq documentation explicitly contrasts mechanical disks—where head-position ordering addressed random-access cost—with solid-state devices that have no mechanical parts and can process requests in parallel.

{/* Source tracking: Host multiqueue motivation and the HDD/SSD distinction were verified against Linux blk-mq documentation; current NVMe queue terminology was checked against the NVM Express specification site. */}

### Counterexample: LBA sorting can reduce parallelism

Suppose SSD requests at LBAs 100–107 map internally across eight channels, while LBAs 108–115 happen to contend on one flash die after FTL remapping. A host policy that assumes the second run is “more sequential” has no evidence for physical service cost. Issuing enough independent work and observing completions may exploit parallelism better than serializing by LBA. The exact mapping is usually controller-private, so the claim must be measurement-driven.

## Queue depth, throughput, and latency obey a conservation relation

For a stable observed system, Little's law relates long-run average outstanding requests $N$, effective completion rate $\lambda$, and average time in the system $W$:

$$
N=\lambda W.
$$

{/* Source tracking: Conditions and meaning of N=lambda W were checked against John D. C. Little's 1961 primary proof; it is used as an observation identity, not a complete latency model. */}

At 100,000 completed operations/s and 400 µs mean end-to-end storage time,

$$
N=100{,}000\times0.0004=40
$$

requests are outstanding on average. A queue cap of 16 cannot coexist with both measured values in a stable closed domain; at least one of rate, latency, scope, or measurement is different. Little's law does not say that setting queue depth to 40 will cause 100,000 IOPS. Service capacity and arrival behavior determine whether that operating point is reachable.

### Mean latency does not protect the tail

Two devices can both average 400 µs while one completes every request near 400 µs and the other pauses during garbage collection. Report p50, p95/p99, maximum within a defined run, and the full workload conditions. A scheduler that improves mean throughput while multiplying p99 latency may violate an interactive-service objective.

**Application — audit a benchmark.** A report claims 150,000 IOPS at 600 µs mean latency with “queue depth 32.” Use Little's law to identify the inconsistency and list two possible scope explanations.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the audit</summary>

The two measured values imply average outstanding work `150,000 × 0.0006 = 90`, not 32. Possible explanations include 32 queues each with more than one outstanding request, a per-thread queue-depth label while 90 requests exist system-wide, latency measured over a broader layer than the reported queue, or throughput and latency taken from different intervals. The result may be valid, but the term “queue depth 32” is underspecified.

</details>

## Boundary cases that defeat one-number policies

### Writes have ordering and durability semantics

A scheduler may reorder independent writes but must preserve barriers and dependencies expressed by upper layers and supported by the device. Completion to software can mean accepted, cached, or durable depending on the protocol and flags. A filesystem cannot infer power-loss durability from queue order alone.

### Virtual disks conceal another scheduler

A guest sees logical blocks presented by a virtual device. The host may map them to a file, network store, SSD, or thin-provisioned volume. Guest cylinder optimization can fight host scheduling. The guest should rely on advertised features and measurement rather than inventing physical geometry.

### Shingled and zoned media alter random-write rules

Some magnetic and solid-state devices expose zones with sequential-write constraints. They are not modeled as ordinary random-write disks. Naming zoned storage without teaching its command/state model would not establish mastery, so it remains outside scope.

### Queue merging can violate latency isolation

Merging adjacent requests lowers per-command overhead, but waiting to form a large merge can delay a small urgent request. Merge windows need bounds tied to service objectives.

## Synthesis assessment: one workload, two media

A server issues this batch of eight 64 KiB reads in arrival order:

```text
90, 10, 92, 12, 160, 14, 162, 16
```

For the magnetic model, the head starts at cylinder 50, valid cylinders are `0..199`, and the only optimized cost is movement. For the SSD model, the device sustains 120,000 reads/s at 300 µs mean latency up to an observed average of 36 outstanding reads; beyond 64 outstanding, p99 latency rises sharply. Two tenants contribute equal request counts, but tenant A has a 2 ms p99 objective while tenant B is bulk work.

Answer:

1. Compute FCFS and SSTF paths and total movement for the magnetic batch.
2. Show an arrival pattern that can starve request 160 under continued SSTF service.
3. Propose a magnetic policy that preserves locality while bounding request age.
4. Verify the SSD operating point with Little's law.
5. Propose an SSD dispatch experiment that protects tenant A and tests whether queue depth 64 is useful.
6. Reject or qualify the claim “sort by LBA on both devices because sequential requests are faster.”

<details>
<summary>Reveal reference solution and rubric</summary>

FCFS path:

```text
50→90→10→92→12→160→14→162→16
```

Movement is `40+80+82+80+148+146+148+146 = 870` cylinders.

SSTF path, with no tied distances in the decisive steps:

```text
50→16→14→12→10→90→92→160→162
```

Movement is `34+2+2+2+80+2+68+2 = 192` cylinders. Continued arrivals near the current low-cylinder position can keep producing requests closer than 160; pure SSTF gives no finite waiting bound. A hybrid keeps address-ordered batches but forces requests whose age reaches a defined threshold, with a batch cap so the check occurs.

For the SSD, `120,000 × 0.0003 = 36`, matching the observed average outstanding count. This validates consistency, not causality. Test queue-depth caps across a sweep below and above 36, isolate tenant A in a weighted/deadline dispatch class, hold the workload and run length constant, and record per-tenant throughput plus p50/p95/p99 latency, queue residence, and device utilization. Stop increasing depth when throughput no longer improves or A's p99 breaches 2 ms; the stated sharp rise beyond 64 makes 64 a ceiling to test, not a target.

LBA sorting has a mechanical rationale for the modeled disk and may aid host merging, but SSD physical placement is hidden by the FTL and the device exposes parallel queues. Use it on SSD only if measurement under the target workload shows a benefit without violating latency/fairness/order objectives.

The SSD discussion describes host-visible scheduling constraints, FTL consequences, and queue observations without claiming a universal internal controller design. RAID, zoned command sets, filesystem allocation, persistent memory, and detailed flash error correction remain outside scope.
